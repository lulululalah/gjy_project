\begin{abstract}
  Complete-aircraft boundary representation (B-Rep) models in Standard for the Exchange of Product model data (STEP) format contain numerous rivets and surface-feature instances, whose reliable recognition is challenged by class imbalance and costly background false positives. The framework represents B-Rep faces as graph nodes and integrates face geometric attributes, adjacency topology, and edge attributes for classification. 
  A dual-specialist GNN (graph neural network) employs independent encoders, classification heads, and decision thresholds for rivets and surface-feature, before fusing the outputs into background, rivet, and surface-feature classes. Training and testing are partitioned by complete aircraft rather than by randomly selected faces, preventing adjacent faces from the same aircraft from appearing in different datasets. The evaluation protocol focuses on per-aircraft errors, 
  background false positives, and maximum false-positive area. The original STEP face-id associated with each prediction is retained during subsequent rivet or surface-feature removal and host-face reconstruction, thereby supporting auditable downstream geometric processing. For large smooth shells, a smooth-shell guard is designed to reduce high-risk background misclassification. The framework supports traceable small-feature recognition and subsequent geometric processing for complete-aircraft B-Rep models,
   while per-aircraft evaluation is used to examine error characteristics and risk boundaries across different models.
%\lipsum[1]
\end{abstract}

{\small\textbf{Keywords}: B-Rep, STEP traceability, graph learning, rivet recognition, surface-features, geometric processing.}

\section{Introduction}

Complete-aircraft STEP/B-Rep models contain many small surface features whose recognition must remain traceable to the original CAD entities. This study develops and evaluates a face-level graph-learning workflow for recognising rivets and other surface-features while controlling the risk of background-face false positives. The method is evaluated using aircraft-level data partitioning and downstream geometry-processing evidence.

%--- Section ---%
\section{Related Work and Problem Formulation}\label{sec2}

Building on the motivation for recognising small surface-features in complete-aircraft STEP models, this section establishes the relevant background and formulates the problem addressed in this work. STEP/B-Rep models encode both geometric entities and their topological relationships; faces, edges, and face adjacency therefore provide a direct basis for face-level analysis \parencite{iso10303_42_2025}. Earlier CAD feature-recognition studies predominantly relied on attributed adjacency graphs, geometric rules, volumetric decomposition, and hint-based reasoning to identify local structures with manufacturing semantics \parencite{han2000manufacturing,shah2001discourse}. More recent work has explored learning directly from B-Rep parameterisations and topology, treating face-level prediction as a graph-learning problem and thereby avoiding some geometric and correspondence losses associated with intermediate mesh or point-cloud representations \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet,wu2024aagnet}.

Complete aircraft differ materially from the mechanical parts commonly considered in public CAD-learning studies. They may contain many faces, extensive continuous curved surfaces, topology that is partitioned by modelling and exchange operations, and substantial variation in external shape, component organisation, and face decomposition across aircraft. Rivets, decals, windows, and related small surface-features are often much less prevalent than background faces, while their local geometry can be similar to that of smooth fuselage or shell regions. Such class imbalance can cause learning and evaluation to be dominated by the majority class \parencite{he2009learning}. Moreover, a false positive on a large background face is not merely a face-level error: when predictions drive feature removal and host-face reconstruction, it can introduce an avoidable downstream geometric-processing risk. Evaluation should therefore consider this risk in addition to aggregate face-level correctness.

Existing public studies primarily address machining features or modelling-operation semantics in mechanical parts. Some formulations jointly consider semantic segmentation, instance grouping, and process-related relations \parencite{lambourne2021brepnet,wu2024aagnet}. Systematic discussion of small surface-feature recognition in complete-aircraft STEP models remains limited. A random face-level train--test split may also place strongly related faces from the same aircraft on both sides of the partition, leading to an optimistic estimate of cross-model generalisation; the distribution of related samples across evaluation partitions is a recognised source of leakage risk in machine-learning studies \parencite{kapoor2023leakage}. In addition, published evaluations commonly focus on segmentation labels or intermediate representations, whereas a traceable evidence chain from predictions to the corresponding STEP face-id is not yet a standard evaluation constraint. Therefore, it is necessary to define the face-level three-class task, specify model-level evaluation constraints, and develop a method that retains face-id traceability for subsequent geometric processing.


\subsection{Face-Level Feature Recognition in STEP/B-Rep Models}

The STEP standard supports the representation of three-dimensional solids using boundary representations (B-Reps), in which topological entities such as vertices, edges, and faces are associated with geometric entities including points, curves, and surfaces. Their connectivity is further expressed through structures such as loops and shells \parencite{iso10303_42_2025}. A face in a STEP model is therefore not an isolated geometric patch: it carries geometric information, such as surface type, area, normal direction, and parameter domain, while also belonging to a topological structure formed through shared edges. Face-level representations provide a traceable unit for subsequent geometric processing, feature localisation, visual annotation, and engineering analysis. They also preserve the correspondence between a prediction and the original B-Rep entity, avoiding sole reliance on meshed or point-cloud approximations that may weaken this correspondence \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet}.

Traditional CAD feature-recognition methods have primarily supported computer-aided process planning and the extraction of manufacturing information. Representative approaches include graph matching, heuristic geometric reasoning, volumetric decomposition and recomposition, and hint-based reasoning \parencite{han2000manufacturing,shah2001discourse}. Rule-based methods commonly identify features such as holes, slots, and bosses by combining predefined geometric thresholds, surface types, concavity or convexity, boundary conditions, and local topological templates. Attributed adjacency graph methods encode faces and their adjacency relations as an attributed graph, and then locate candidate features through subgraph matching or pattern recognition \parencite{babic2008review,han2000manufacturing}. These methods are interpretable and effective when feature definitions, geometric constraints, and topological patterns are well specified. However, feature interactions, domain dependence, multiple valid interpretations, and complex surface geometry increase the difficulty of rule construction and matching \parencite{shah2001discourse}. Consequently, maintaining rule sets, transferring thresholds, and preserving robustness across models can become challenging for models with continuous free-form surfaces, complex local connections, and varying face partitions.

More recently, learning-based B-Rep methods have provided an alternative to explicit rule matching. A B-Rep can be represented as a graph in which faces serve as nodes, shared-edge or adjacency relations serve as edges, and face geometry, edge attributes, and local topology are encoded as node or edge features \parencite{colligan2022hierarchical,jayaraman2021uvnet}. In this representation, graph neural networks can aggregate information from adjacent faces and progressively incorporate a wider topological context, framing face-level classification as node classification on a graph \parencite{lambourne2021brepnet}. UV-Net combines geometric information from the parametric domains of surfaces and curves with adjacency-graph topology, whereas BRepNet performs topological message passing over faces, edges, and oriented coedges \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet}. For machining feature recognition, Hierarchical CADNet similarly encodes face adjacency, surface geometry, and edge convexity to predict a feature class for each B-Rep face \parencite{colligan2022hierarchical}.

Nevertheless, representative studies have largely considered mechanical CAD models, synthetic B-Rep samples, or machining-feature data, where the semantic classes are commonly associated with modelling operations or manufacturing features such as holes, slots, and bosses \parencite{colligan2022hierarchical,jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet}. The complete-aircraft STEP models considered in this work instead involve an aircraft-scale setting, a high proportion of continuous free-form surfaces, complex structural hierarchy, and substantial variation in face decomposition and feature distribution across aircraft. Rivets often correspond to small and densely distributed local face sets, while surface-features may span different scales and curvature regions; both must be distinguished from large, smooth background regions. These differences indicate that face-level small-feature recognition on complete-aircraft B-Rep graphs, particularly for rivets and surface-features, warrants further systematic investigation.

Accordingly, existing studies do not directly resolve the three-class face-level task considered here, namely assigning every original B-Rep face to \textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, or \textit{surface-feature}. The task requires not only generalisation across complete aircraft and aircraft types, but also stable traceability from predictions to the original STEP face-id for human review, visual annotation, and downstream engineering workflows. In addition, falsely labelling a large smooth background face as a feature may create a material engineering risk; aggregate classification measures alone are therefore insufficient to characterise recognition quality. The next subsection discusses the specific challenges of small surface-feature recognition in complete-aircraft models.

\subsection{Challenges and Problem Formulation}\label{subsec1}

Small surface-feature recognition in complete-aircraft STEP models is not solely a face-level classification problem. It is jointly constrained by class distribution, geometric and topological variation, cross-aircraft generalisation, and the engineering consequences of downstream geometric processing. The following subsections define these challenges and formulate the task considered in this work.

\subsubsection{Class Imbalance and Scale Variation}\label{subsubsec:class_imbalance}

Small surface-feature recognition in complete-aircraft STEP models first faces pronounced scale and class imbalance. Compared with background faces that constitute the main body of the fuselage, wings, and engine nacelles, rivets usually correspond to only a small number of local faces with limited area. The \textit{surface-feature} class may include windows, decals, and other surface-attached structures, whose geometric scale, shape, and spatial distribution are more variable. Consequently, background faces commonly dominate both face count and surface coverage; model training and aggregate evaluation can therefore be dominated by the majority class, while missed detections and false detections of minority classes may be obscured by overall accuracy. This is consistent with the general challenge in imbalanced learning, where the majority class can dominate the decision boundary and reduce recognition of minority classes \parencite{he2009learning}.

\subsubsection{Free-Form Surfaces and B-Rep Face-Partition Variation}\label{subsubsec:brep_variation}

The continuous free-form surfaces of complete aircraft mean that the geometric boundary between a small feature and its background is not always explicit. A rivet may appear as a local protrusion, a depression, or a small structure composed of multiple adjacent faces, whereas a \textit{surface-feature} may have planar, curved, or mixed boundary forms. In addition, different STEP exporters, modelling histories, and Boolean operations can alter the face partition of a similar local structure, producing variation in face count, adjacency pattern, and surface parameterisation. It is therefore difficult to reliably distinguish a true feature from local geometric variation on a fuselage or wing using only the area, surface type, or curvature of an individual face. Recognition must instead consider face adjacency, local topological context, and a wider structural context.

\subsubsection{Cross-Aircraft Generalisation, Traceability, and Engineering Risk}\label{subsubsec:generalisation_traceability}

Generalisation across complete aircraft is an important constraint of this task. If training and test sets are split randomly by individual faces or local subgraphs, geometrically similar and topologically related faces from the same aircraft may occur in both partitions. Test results may then partly reflect reproduction of already observed whole-aircraft structures rather than generalisation to unseen aircraft. Machine-learning research has shown that dependence between training and test samples can lead to data leakage and overly optimistic performance estimates \parencite{kapoor2023leakage}. Accordingly, this work uses the complete aircraft as the basic unit of data partitioning: training and test aircraft are independent at the model level, so that evaluation better reflects the intended use on unseen complete-aircraft models.

The task output is intended not only for semantic annotation but also for subsequent geometric processing and human review. Each prediction should remain traceable to its original STEP face-id, enabling the corresponding face to be located, visualised, and verified on the original B-Rep. False positives on large smooth background faces require particular attention. If such a face is incorrectly labelled as a \textit{rivet} or \textit{surface-feature} and subsequently participates in feature removal, host-face reconstruction, or other geometric operations, it may have a disproportionate effect on downstream model integrity. Therefore, in addition to conventional face-level classification metrics, evaluation should examine background faces incorrectly identified as features for each aircraft and the areas of the affected faces, so as to more fully characterise the engineering risk of the recognition result.

\subsection{Limitations of Existing Methods and Research Gaps}\label{subsec:research_gaps}

Existing B-Rep learning studies have established the feasibility of face-level prediction from geometric and topological information. BRepNet performs topological message passing over faces, edges, and oriented coedges for modelling-operation segmentation, while Hierarchical CADNet combines B-Rep face adjacency, surface geometry, and edge attributes for machining-feature recognition \parencite{lambourne2021brepnet,colligan2022hierarchical}. These studies provide relevant methodological foundations, but their label systems and evaluation objectives are not identical to the task considered in this work.

Published B-Rep learning benchmarks primarily use modelling-operation labels or machining-feature classes. They do not directly define the three-class task of distinguishing \textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, and \textit{surface-feature} faces in complete-aircraft STEP models \parencite{lambourne2021brepnet,colligan2022hierarchical}. In particular, existing evidence does not establish whether a unified classifier or separately trained predictors is more suitable for the two target feature categories. The dual-specialist GNN in this work is therefore treated as a testable architectural choice rather than as a consequence already demonstrated by prior studies. Its value must be assessed through a controlled comparison with a unified three-class model under the same data split and training budget.

Evaluation protocols also require careful alignment with the intended deployment setting. If related samples are shared between training and test partitions, the resulting evaluation may provide an overly optimistic estimate of performance on independent objects. For the present task, the complete aircraft is consequently used as the unit of data partitioning, so that the evaluation addresses transfer to unseen aircraft rather than recognition of related faces from an already observed model.

In addition, existing studies commonly evaluate semantic segmentation or machining-feature labels at the face level. The present workflow requires each prediction to remain linked to the corresponding original STEP face-id, because the output is subsequently used for feature removal and host-face reconstruction. Face-level classification scores alone do not establish this geometry-processing traceability. Retaining the original face-id therefore forms an explicit output constraint in this work and provides an auditable link from the predicted label to the B-Rep entity used in subsequent operations.

These considerations define the scope of the proposed method: the dual-specialist GNN tests task-specific prediction design, aircraft-level partitioning supports an evaluation aligned with the intended use case, and face-id retention connects face-level predictions to subsequent geometric processing. The following subsection formalises the corresponding graph representation and classification task.

\subsection{Face-Level Three-Class Problem Formulation}\label{subsec:problem_formulation}

Accordingly, each complete-aircraft STEP model is represented as a face-level graph $G=(V,E)$, where the node set $V$ corresponds to original B-Rep faces and the edge set $E$ represents face adjacency. Nodes and edges carry attributes derived from faces, edges, and their local topology. For each node $v_i$, the objective is to predict a class label $y_i \in \{\textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, \textit{surface-feature}\}$ while preserving a one-to-one correspondence between the prediction and the original STEP face-id. This formulation jointly incorporates cross-aircraft generalisation, three-class face-level recognition, prediction traceability, and the risk of false positives on large background faces, thereby establishing the basis for the subsequent method design and evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Overview of the Proposed Method and Contributions}\label{subsec:method_overview}

To address the recognition, geometric simplification, and simulation-acceleration requirements associated with small surface-features in complete-aircraft STEP/B-Rep models, this work proposes a face-level graph-learning framework that integrates cross-aircraft generalisation, engineering-risk control, and geometric traceability. Each complete aircraft is represented as a B-Rep face graph whose nodes are faces and whose edges encode topological adjacency; face geometric attributes, topological relations, and edge attributes are incorporated as input information. Beyond semantic recognition, the framework is intended to support the location and removal of non-critical small features, thereby reducing unnecessary mesh complexity and supporting more efficient simulation preparation and numerical analysis.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item A face-level B-Rep graph representation is constructed for three-class prediction: \textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, and \textit{surface-feature}. The \textit{surface-feature} class encompasses non-rivet small surface-features, including decals and windows.
  \item A dual-specialist GNN architecture is developed, comprising a rivet specialist and a surface-feature specialist. The two specialists learn the respective discrimination patterns of the target feature categories, and their predictions are fused at the final stage.
  \item Training and testing are partitioned by complete aircraft rather than by randomly selected faces. In addition to conventional classification measures, the evaluation considers, for each aircraft, the number of background faces falsely identified as features and the maximum area of a falsely identified background face. These risk-oriented measures address the engineering consequence of misclassifying large smooth shell faces.
  \item The original STEP face-id is retained for every prediction, allowing the predicted label to be traced back to a specific geometric entity. This traceability supports subsequent feature removal, host-face localisation, and host-face reconstruction, and provides an entity-level basis for geometric simplification.
  \item A protection mechanism is introduced for large smooth shell background faces to reduce the risk of high-impact background misclassification. It is intended to preserve critical exterior geometry during feature processing while supporting mesh-count reduction and simulation acceleration.
\end{enumerate}

The next section describes the data preparation, graph representation, dual-specialist GNN, decision rules, and geometry-processing procedure used for the evaluation.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data preparation and label contract}\label{sec:data_preparation}

This study treats each complete-aircraft STEP model as the fundamental data unit rather than treating individual B-Rep faces or local subgraphs as mutually independent samples. The STEP standard supports the representation of solids as boundary representations (B-Reps), in which topological entities, including faces, edges, and vertices, are associated with geometric entities and linked through structures such as loops and shells \parencite{iso10303_42_2025}. For each model, the original B-Rep faces and their face-ids are extracted from the STEP file and retained throughout feature extraction, training, inference, prediction export, and geometric processing. The final STEP models used for prediction--truth comparison are stored separately from intermediate STEP outputs generated during rivet or surface-feature removal, so that intermediate geometric modifications cannot alter face-ids or topology and compromise the consistency of the evaluation object.

The face-level recognition task uses a three-class label contract: background, rivet, and surface-feature. Background denotes a conventional B-Rep face that is not part of a target small feature; rivet denotes a face forming part of a local rivet structure; and surface-feature denotes a non-rivet small feature that requires consideration in subsequent simplification or reconstruction, including decals and windows. The three classes are encoded as 0, 1, and 2, respectively, for model training and result exchange. Decal and window provenance may be retained in the original annotation records, but both are assigned to the surface-feature class for face-level classification and evaluation, thereby maintaining a consistent three-class task definition.

Training, inference, and evaluation records use the original STEP face-id as the primary key. Each prediction file stores the face-id, predicted class, and class name; when confidence output is required, the corresponding prediction score is also retained. Before face-level evaluation, the final STEP model, prediction CSV, and truth CSV are subjected to an alignment check. They must contain exactly the same number of faces, prediction face-ids must be unique, and the face-id sets covered by the predictions and truth labels must each correspond one-to-one with the face set of the final STEP model. A model that does not satisfy these conditions is excluded from metric calculation and subsequent geometric processing, preventing file mismatches, changed face numbering, or incomplete predictions from being interpreted as recognition errors.

The dataset is partitioned at the complete-aircraft model level. Faces and local adjacency relations from the same aircraft are permitted in only one partition; thus, the training and test sets do not share same-aircraft faces, adjacent faces, or whole-aircraft structures. Dependence between related training and test samples can cause data leakage and overly optimistic estimates of model performance \parencite{kapoor2023leakage}. The current split contains 17 complete-aircraft models for training and 9 for testing. The training set is used only to learn model parameters and to compute normalisation statistics for node and edge attributes. Test models and subsequent STEP inputs reuse the stored statistics, feature-column order, class mapping, architecture parameters, and decision thresholds without recalculation from the test object. The test set is used only for independent evaluation after the training protocol has been fixed and does not participate in checkpoint selection or threshold adjustment. \subsection{Face graph and input attributes}\label{sec:face_graph}

Section~\ref{sec:data_preparation} defines the three-class label contract and requires every label and prediction to remain aligned with an original STEP face-id. This section specifies how that contract is converted into a learnable face-level graph. A graph node is an original B-Rep face rather than a geometric vertex or a mesh facet, so it remains a locatable and reviewable CAD entity throughout feature extraction and inference. This choice retains the correspondence between a prediction and the source B-Rep entity instead of associating the prediction only with an approximate region produced by a converted representation. \mbox{\textcite{yeo2021machining}} similarly showed that face-based B-Rep descriptors can link learning-based feature recognition directly to original B-Rep entities. Their machining-feature task is distinct from the aircraft setting considered here, but it supports the use of face entities as the unit through which recognition outputs remain traceable.

For each complete-aircraft STEP model, the face-level graph is denoted by $G=(V,E)$. Each node $v_i\in V$ corresponds to one original B-Rep face and retains its STEP face-id; an edge in $E$ records adjacency when two faces share a boundary. Thus, graph connectivity is obtained from the STEP/B-Rep topology rather than introduced by a spatial-distance threshold. The graph separates information about an individual face from information about its relationship with neighbouring faces: the former is stored as node attributes and the latter as edge attributes. B-Rep learning studies likewise use graph representations to organise geometric entities together with their topological relations for CAD learning tasks \parencite{zhang2024brep2seq}. This work adopts that representational principle without assuming that the feature classes, architecture, or evaluation setting of prior CAD studies transfer directly to complete-aircraft models.

The current implementation assigns 48 node attributes to each B-Rep face. These attributes are grouped by information source rather than interpreted as hand-coded recognition rules. They include measures of face scale and shape, such as relative area, perimeter, compactness, and relations to neighbouring-face areas; surface and curvature information, including curvature, radius availability, and surface type; boundary-organisation measures, including wire, inner-wire, and edge counts; and local-neighbourhood statistics describing adjacent surface types, convex, concave, or smooth relations, normal similarity, and same-surface connectivity. Model-scale-normalised geometric quantities and relative positional descriptors are also included. The complete attribute vector therefore characterises the local state of a B-Rep face, but does not prescribe that a particular area, surface type, or curvature value must be classified as \textit{rivet} or \textit{surface-feature}.

This organisation is necessary for two reasons. First, \textit{rivet} instances commonly occur as local structures with repeated patterns and relatively clear boundaries, whereas \textit{surface-feature} instances may differ substantially in scale, surface form, and attachment mode. A single face area or surface type is consequently insufficient to distinguish these target classes reliably from \textit{background}. Second, complete-aircraft models vary in physical scale and B-Rep face partitioning, so unprocessed geometric magnitudes can inadvertently encode model size or export conventions rather than class-relevant evidence. Geometric quantities requiring scale consistency are therefore normalised using the model-scale convention, and graph inference reuses the feature-column order and statistics determined from the training set. This normalisation is a data-processing convention of the present method; it is not evidence that the resulting representation will generalise to arbitrary aircraft sources.

Each face adjacency is associated with six edge attributes: connection type, area ratio, neighbouring surface type, shared-edge length, mean dihedral angle, and dihedral-angle standard deviation. These attributes describe the boundary relation between two B-Rep faces rather than either face in isolation. For example, shared-edge length characterises the local scale of an adjacency, dihedral-angle statistics describe local turning or continuity, and neighbouring surface type and area ratio provide relational information unavailable from a single node. Edge-conditioned graph convolutions provide a general basis for retaining such information during neighbourhood aggregation, because their filters can be conditioned on edge labels \parencite{simonovsky2017dynamic}. In the present work, this observation motivates supplying edge attributes to message passing; the specific dual-specialist encoder and its learned edge-conditioned operations are defined in the next subsection.

The resulting graph input consists jointly of the node-attribute matrix and the edge-attribute set. Message passing does not replace the B-Rep topology; rather, it updates a node representation by combining its own attributes with the attributes of topologically adjacent faces and their connecting relations. A candidate face can therefore be assessed in local geometric--topological context instead of only through its individual area, curvature, or surface type. No single input column is treated as a fixed rule for either target class. The rivet and surface-feature specialists described in Section~\ref{sec:dual_gnn} learn different combinations of these shared input attributes.

Finally, the mapping between graph-node indices and STEP face-id values is retained throughout graph construction. Continuous node indices may be used for tensor operations, but exported predictions are restored to the original STEP face-id so that they can be aligned with truth labels, visually reviewed on the source model, and passed to subsequent geometry-processing steps. The label contract from Section~\ref{sec:data_preparation} is therefore realised here as a graph-input contract: \textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, and \textit{surface-feature} are node labels; node and edge attributes are learning inputs; and STEP face-id is the persistent entity identifier connecting data preparation, inference, and audit.

\subsection{Dual-specialist graph neural network}\label{sec:dual_gnn}

The proposed classifier contains a rivet specialist and a surface-feature specialist. Both specialists receive the face graph and the node and edge attributes defined in Section~\ref{sec:face_graph}, but they do not share node encoders, edge encoders, graph-convolution layers, adapters, or output heads. The rivet specialist therefore learns a background--rivet decision, whereas the surface-feature specialist learns a background--surface-feature decision. This separation is intended to reduce interference between target classes with different geometric patterns and error consequences; it does not assume a performance advantage before comparative experiments. The use of task-specific representations is conceptually related to multi-task learning, but the present model does not use a shared gate or a mixture-of-experts module.

Figure~\ref{fig:dual_specialist_gnn} summarises the architecture. It shows the shared B-Rep face-graph input, the two parameter-independent branches, the score-level decision fusion, and the STEP face-id traceability path. The traceability path is an entity-identifier contract between graph construction and prediction export; it is not an additional neural-network input feature.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_dual_specialist_gnn_architecture.png}
\caption{Dual-specialist GNN for face-level three-class prediction with STEP face-id traceability. The same B-Rep face graph, node attributes, and edge attributes enter parameter-independent rivet and surface-feature specialists. Each branch contains separate attribute encoders, four residual edge-conditioned graph-convolution layers, an adapter, and an output head. The two sigmoid scores are fused using rivet precedence to produce \textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, or \textit{surface-feature}. The dashed traceability path connects the source face entity and exported prediction, but is not a learned feature.}
\label{fig:dual_specialist_gnn}
\end{figure}

For specialist $k\in\{r,s\}$, where $r$ denotes rivet and $s$ denotes surface-feature, the raw node and edge attributes are first transformed by separate encoders:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(0)}=\phi_{x,k}(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{ij,k}=\phi_{e,k}(\mathbf{e}_{ij}).
\label{eq:dual_specialist_input_encoding}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf{x}_i$ is the 48-dimensional attribute vector of B-Rep face node $v_i$, $\mathbf{e}_{ij}$ is the six-dimensional attribute vector of the adjacency between faces $i$ and $j$, and $\phi_{x,k}$ and $\phi_{e,k}$ are the specialist-specific node and edge encoders. Each encoder comprises a linear transformation, LayerNorm, and ReLU activation. The input attributes are identical for the two specialists, but the parameters of $\phi_{x,r}$, $\phi_{e,r}$, $\phi_{x,s}$, and $\phi_{e,s}$ are distinct.

Each branch then applies four edge-conditioned graph-convolution layers with hidden dimension 64. At layer $\ell$, the encoded edge attributes condition the message transformation used by \texttt{NNConv}; the updated node state is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_{i,k}^{(\ell+1)}=
\operatorname{Dropout}\!\left(
\operatorname{ReLU}\!\left(
\operatorname{LN}\!\left(
\operatorname{NNConv}_{\ell,k}
(\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(\ell)},\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{ij,k})
\right)\right)\right).
\label{eq:dual_specialist_message_update}
\end{equation}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:dual_specialist_message_update}, $\operatorname{NNConv}_{\ell,k}$ denotes the edge-conditioned graph convolution in branch $k$, $\operatorname{LN}$ is LayerNorm, and $\mathbf{u}_{i,k}^{(\ell+1)}$ is the post-activation and post-dropout update. Edge-conditioned filters provide a general mechanism for conditioning graph-convolution weights on discrete or continuous edge labels \parencite{simonovsky2017dynamic}. More generally, the formulation follows the message-passing view of graph learning \parencite{gilmer2017neural}; neither source defines the aircraft-specific attributes, thresholds, or decision rule used here.

The first graph-convolution layer uses $\mathbf{u}_{i,k}^{(1)}$ directly. The remaining layers use residual connections,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(\ell+1)}=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{u}_{i,k}^{(1)}, & \ell=0,\\
\mathbf{u}_{i,k}^{(\ell+1)}+\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(\ell)}, & \ell\geq1.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:dual_specialist_residual}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(\ell)}$ is the node representation before the $\ell$th update. This implementation preserves the preceding representation while allowing subsequent layers to incorporate a wider topological context. Residual graph-convolution designs provide relevant general motivation for this operation \parencite{li2019deepgcns}, although the present architecture uses four layers rather than the deep networks evaluated in that study.

After the fourth layer, the implementation concatenates each node representation with the mean-pooled and max-pooled representation of its complete-aircraft graph. With the current four-layer, 64-dimensional setting, this yields a 192-dimensional specialist representation, which is mapped by a 96-dimensional adapter containing a linear layer, LayerNorm, ReLU, and dropout. The resulting output head produces one logit for each face. Let $\mathbf{z}_{i,r}$ and $\mathbf{z}_{i,s}$ denote the two adapter outputs. The independent sigmoid scores are
\begin{equation}
p_{r,i}=\sigma(g_r(\mathbf{z}_{i,r})), \qquad
p_{s,i}=\sigma(g_s(\mathbf{z}_{i,s})),
\label{eq:dual_specialist_scores}
\end{equation}
where $g_r$ and $g_s$ are the rivet and surface-feature output heads, respectively, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function. Direct learning from B-Rep topology and geometry has been studied for CAD tasks \parencite{lambourne2021brepnet,colligan2022hierarchical,wu2024aagnet}; however, those studies use different datasets and label definitions, so they are methodological context rather than replications of the present task.

The two scores are converted to the final face label by decision fusion:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i =
\begin{cases}
\textit{rivet}, & p_{r,i} \geq \tau_r, \\
\textit{surface-feature}, & p_{r,i} < \tau_r \ \text{and} \ p_{s,i} \geq \tau_s, \\
\textit{background}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:dual_head_decision}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}_i$ is the final class of face $i$, and $\tau_r$ and $\tau_s$ are the rivet and surface-feature thresholds. The rivet decision has precedence: once $p_{r,i}$ reaches $\tau_r$, the surface-feature score cannot overwrite that prediction. The current inference contract uses $\tau_r=0.9$ and $\tau_s=0.5$; the paired model statistics file must be retained with the checkpoint when this contract is reproduced. The fused labels and their probabilities are subsequently passed to the training, inference, and smooth-shell protection procedures described in Section~\ref{sec:training_inference}.

\subsection{Training and inference protocol}\label{sec:training_inference}

Building on the dual-specialist GNN and decision-fusion rule defined in Section~\ref{sec:dual_gnn}, this section specifies a unified protocol for model training, checkpoint preservation, and prediction export. Each complete-aircraft STEP model is represented as one face-level graph and used as an independent training sample; faces and local topological neighbourhoods from the same aircraft do not cross the training and test partitions. Normalisation statistics for node and edge attributes are computed from the training set only and retained together with the feature-column order, class mapping, network configuration, and decision thresholds. For a test aircraft, inference reuses these frozen statistics and feature columns without recalculating preprocessing parameters or adapting the classification rule to the test object. This protocol maintains a consistent graph-representation contract between training and inference and prevents test data from determining preprocessing configuration \parencite{kapoor2023leakage}.

The two specialists are jointly optimised on the same complete-aircraft graphs, while learning separate binary decisions for background--rivet and background--surface-feature classification. Each output head computes a class-aware focal loss, and the two objectives update parameter-independent encoders, graph-convolution layers, adapters, and classification heads within the same optimisation process. Class-aware weighting configures positive and negative sample weights from the training-set class distribution, thereby reducing the dominance of numerous background faces; the focal term further decreases the contribution of already well-classified samples and retains emphasis on minority-class and difficult-background samples. This mechanism is part of the training loss rather than a rule applied after prediction \parencite{lin2017focal,buda2018imbalance}.

The reported configuration uses AdamW with an initial learning rate of 0.005, dropout of 0.2, weight decay of $10^{-4}$, random seed 123, and 50 training epochs over full-aircraft graphs with a batch size of 1. AdamW decouples weight decay from adaptive gradient updates \parencite{loshchilov2019adamw}. After training, model weights, training-set normalisation statistics, node and edge feature-column order, the three-class label mapping, and inference-relevant architecture parameters are retained as a paired checkpoint bundle. The test partition does not participate in parameter updates, threshold selection, or checkpoint selection; it is evaluated only after the training protocol has been fixed.

At inference, a STEP model is converted into a face-level graph using the training-time feature-column order, while the one-to-one correspondence between each B-Rep face node and its original STEP face-id is retained. The two specialists output rivet and surface-feature scores, which are converted to final three-class labels using the decision-fusion rule in Section~\ref{sec:dual_gnn}. A face is labelled as \textit{rivet} when its rivet score reaches the fixed threshold of 0.9; it is labelled as \textit{surface-feature} only when the rivet condition is not satisfied and its surface-feature score reaches 0.5; otherwise, it is labelled as \textit{background}. Thus, threshold fusion is part of the dual-specialist classification decision rather than an external post-processing step, and the rivet decision has precedence. Prediction exports retain face-id, the final class, the class name, and the corresponding specialist scores, enabling each output to be traced to a specific STEP face and aligned face by face with truth labels or subsequent geometry operations.

After decision fusion, the inference procedure may apply conservative structural risk constraints. The rivet constraint acts only on candidates already predicted as \textit{rivet}: it contracts a candidate to \textit{background} when the face is planar, has at least four planar neighbours, and has no smooth boundary edge. The surface-feature constraint acts only on candidates already predicted as \textit{surface-feature}. It may contract a candidate according to its structural role within a large, smooth same-surface connected component and evaluates the exterior visibility of every remaining candidate from sampled surface points. A candidate is returned to \textit{background} only when all sampled points are occluded along the prescribed directions; a face for which the geometric check fails is retained. Neither constraint alters the dual-specialist GNN input features or retrains the model, and neither promotes background faces to a target class or uses a global hard threshold on individual face area. Their role is limited to constraining downstream use of specific high-risk candidates, rather than evaluating or substituting for the classification capability of the dual-specialist GNN.

\subsection{Feature removal and surface-mesh protocol}\label{sec:geometry_protocol}

The predictions output by Section~3.4 associate face-level labels with specific entities in the input B-Rep through STEP face-id. This section treats that association as the input contract for geometry processing and specifies how predictions are converted into traceable objects for feature removal, local reconstruction, and surface-mesh evaluation. Face-level prediction correctness, successful feature removal or local reconstruction, and suitability of the simplified model for a downstream engineering task are three distinct levels of evidence. Evidence at one level does not automatically establish the next. In particular, successful STEP re-reading or a passed B-Rep check establishes only the relevant data-exchange or geometric-validity condition; it does not establish lossless simplification, simulation equivalence, or unchanged engineering performance. Whether a CAD simplification is acceptable also depends on the analysis task, loads, boundary conditions, and functional regions that must be retained \parencite{thakur2009cad_simplification,antolin2024analysis_aware_defeaturing}.

For each input STEP model, the program reads a prediction CSV containing a face-id and \texttt{pred-label}, and checks field completeness and face-id uniqueness. A face-id has direct localisation meaning only in the source STEP that produced the prediction. Removal, sewing, surface extension, and local reconstruction can change both topology and face enumeration; the original face-ids are therefore not assumed to remain invariant in subsequent intermediate STEP files. The workflow consequently processes rivets and surface-features in separate stages. It first locates and removes faces predicted as \textit{rivet}; it then re-extracts face geometry and topology from the rivet-removed STEP model and performs a second \textit{surface-feature} prediction. This order bases the second-stage prediction on the updated local geometry rather than on initial face attributes that may have been changed by rivet removal. Accordingly, the second-stage CSV establishes a new face-id contract that applies only to its corresponding intermediate STEP model.

The second-stage prediction is generated from the rivet-removed intermediate STEP model and therefore uses a new face-id contract. Figure~\ref{fig:geometry_protocol} summarises this staged prediction, geometry-processing, and validation contract. For a \textit{surface-feature} identified in the second stage, the workflow first analyses the local geometric and topological relation between the target face and its adjacent host faces, and then selects an operation from a unified strategy set. Generic feature removal is used when the target can be suppressed while preserving local boundary consistency. Host-face local reconstruction is used when removing an embedded target requires recovery of the surrounding surface region. Split/bridge repair is used when the local region contains split boundaries or a risk of disconnected repair. All strategies share the same input--output contract, exception handling, STEP re-readability check, and B-Rep validity check. If a candidate operation does not produce an acceptable geometric result, it is routed to rollback or manual review and is not written as a final checked STEP.

Candidate geometric results are subjected to STEP re-reading and B-Rep validity checks. If an operation fails, the output model cannot be read, or the check does not meet the current processing requirement, the candidate is not treated as a final output and is instead routed to rollback or manual review. The original verification STEP, intermediate removal STEP, and final checked STEP are stored separately: the original model retains the source of predictions and the reference for later comparison; intermediate models support staged feature extraction and strategy execution; and a final model corresponds only to an output that has passed the specified checks. OpenCASCADE's \texttt{BRepCheck-Analyzer} checks whether a shape and its subshapes satisfy the kernel's B-Rep validity criteria \parencite{occt_brepcheck_2026}. This check supports reporting model readability and geometric validity, but it does not replace validation of physical response.

Surface-mesh evaluation is conducted only for original--simplified STEP pairs that have both been generated successfully and can be re-read. Both endpoints use the same OpenCASCADE surface mesher and parameters: a relative linear deflection of 0.0005 and an angular deflection of 0.5 rad. The relative linear deflection controls the permitted deviation between curves or surfaces and their discrete approximations according to model scale, whereas angular deflection limits the turning angle between successive discrete segments \parencite{occt_mesh_2026}. These mesh parameters are distinct from, and must not be confused with, the prohibited prediction-stage hard area threshold of 0.0005. Each model is meshed five times and the median mesh-generation time is recorded. The evaluation also records mesh node count, triangle count, degenerate-triangle count, minimum angle and its quantiles, and aspect ratio and its quantiles. Table~\ref{tab:geometry_protocol} summarises these settings and recorded quantities. These indicators describe mesh scale, discretisation quality, and potential quality risk, but cannot alone establish finite-element solution accuracy or convergence \parencite{sorgente2023mesh_quality}.

To compare geometric differences between the original and simplified models, surface points are sampled from both endpoint meshes, and point-to-shape distances are computed from the original surface to the simplified surface and in the reverse direction. Bidirectional sampling avoids omitting a local deviation on the opposite surface when only one direction is evaluated \parencite{aspert2002mesh}. The two directions have different interpretations. Distances from the original model to the simplified model include intentionally removed rivets and surface-features and therefore cannot be interpreted simply as a global error of the main shell. Distances from the simplified model to the original model describe the nearest-surface difference for retained or reconstructed regions, but likewise cannot replace simulation validation under specified loads, boundary conditions, and material properties. If either endpoint has free edges, volume is not treated as a comparable metric; relative volume change is recorded only when both endpoints satisfy the required closed-shape condition.

This section therefore defines a reproducible protocol from face-level predictions to CAD geometry processing, B-Rep checking, and surface-mesh comparison. The protocol supports recording prediction-face traceability, removal and reconstruction operations, output isolation, mesh size, mesh quality, and sampled geometric differences. However, before uniform statistics of target-removal completeness, a failure-case summary, and controlled CAE comparisons are available, it does not support claims that all targets have been successfully removed, all host faces can be reconstructed automatically, simplification is lossless, or mesh-count reduction necessarily yields proportional solver acceleration or equivalent physical response.

Each endpoint was meshed five times, and the median mesh-generation time was recorded. The authors must declare conflicts of interest or state ``The authors declare no conflict of interest.'' Authors must identify and declare any personal circumstances or interests that may be perceived as inappropriately influencing the representation or interpretation of reported research results. A detailed definition of conflicts of interest is available at the following site: \url{https://academic.oup.com/journals/pages/authors/preparing_your_manuscript/ethics#conflict}.

\section*{Author contributions}
The authors must specify the individual contributions of all authors, identified by full names, according to NISO CrediT (Contributer Roles Taxonomy) described at the following site: \url{https://credit.niso.org/}. An example statement is as follows:

\noindent\textbf{Kunwoo Lee}: Conceptualization, Methodology, Software. \textbf{Shuming Gao}: Data curation, Writing---original draft. \textbf{Sang Hun Lee}: Visualization, Investigation. \textbf{Jami J. Shah}: Supervision. \textbf{Hiromasa Suzuki}: Software, Validation. \textbf{Myung-Il Roh}: Writing---review \& editing.

\section*{Funding}
Cite all funding for your research, providing the grant number and the funder name. An example statement is as follows: This work is supported in part by funds from the National Science Foundation (NSF: \# 1636933 and \# 1920920). 

If the funder is listed in the \href{https://www.crossref.org/services/funder-registry/}{Crossref Funder Registry}, the funder name should appear exactly as it does in that database. Where grants were received by specific members of the author group, they should be identified by initials.

More information on funding agency requirements is available from the \href{https://academic.oup.com/pages/open-research/open-access/complying-with-funder-policies}{Oxford Academic funding-policy guidance}.

\section{Section title of first appendix}
Appendices are used for supplementary material that is relevant to the main text but not essential for inclusion in the text itself---for example, questionnaires, interview transcripts, extended data tables, or equipment descriptions.
\begin{itemize}
\item If there is only one appendix, label it as ``Appendix''; if there are multiple appendices, label them as ``Appendix A,'' ``Appendix B,'' and so on, arranging them in the order they appear in the paper.
\item All appendices must be referenced in the main text, and only items that enhance the reader's understanding or support your argument should be included.
\item Tables and figures in an appendix should be labeled separately (e.g., Table \ref{tab_a1}, Figure \ref{fig:cat2}).
\end{itemize}
