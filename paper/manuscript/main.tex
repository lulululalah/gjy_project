\begin{abstract}
Locating rivet and cosmetic-feature faces in aircraft CAD models requires geometric context and a connection to editable boundary representation (B-Rep) entities. Target regions vary in surface form and face partitioning, while removal changes the topology on which recognition operates. We present a face graph framework that recognises targets directly in the native B-Rep and connects predictions to staged geometric processing. An edge-conditioned graph neural network combines local face relations with aircraft-level context to predict background, rivet, and cosmetic-feature labels. Graph re-extraction and geometric correspondence maintain candidate references between rivet processing and cosmetic-feature reconstruction. On a developmental set of nine aircraft containing 34,162 faces, the complete recognition system, including geometric candidate processing, achieves F1 scores of 0.9297 for rivets and 0.7776 for cosmetic features. Across all nine processed aircraft, surface-triangle counts decrease by 2.55\%--38.36\% and median mesh-generation times by 1.09\%--61.61\% under fixed tessellation controls. The results document the connection between face-level recognition, editable geometry, and surface-mesh scale.
\end{abstract}

{\small\textbf{Keywords}: B-Rep feature recognition, graph learning, rivet recognition, cosmetic features, geometric processing.}

\section{Introduction}

Detailed aircraft CAD models contain local structures that require separate treatment when preparing geometry for editing or meshing. A rivet may be represented by several small faces, a window by an embedded patch or a partition of the surrounding skin, and a decal by a trimmed surface region. Identifying these structures is therefore a problem of interpreting both surface geometry and its topological context. The output must locate editable B-Rep faces, since a semantic region alone does not specify the entities on which removal or reconstruction should act. Figure~\ref{fig:gulfstream_target_examples} illustrates the target regions considered here.

\begin{figure}[H]
\input{figures/fig_gulfstream_target_examples.tex}
\caption{Annotated target faces on the Gulfstream G280 v17 evaluation B-Rep. (A) Side and (B) top views show the marked regions. Panels (C) and (D) show cosmetic-feature examples 1 (decal region) and 2 (window region), respectively; panel (E) shows a rivet.}\label{fig:gulfstream_target_examples}
\end{figure}

CAD feature recognition has long used attributed adjacency graphs, geometric reasoning, and decomposition to recover manufacturing semantics \parencite{han2000manufacturing,shah2001discourse,babic2008review}. Learning directly from B-Rep entities extends this approach by combining geometry with topology, as demonstrated by UV-Net, BRepNet, Hierarchical CADNet, and AAGNet \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet,colligan2022hierarchical,wu2024aagnet}. Their representations provide a basis for face prediction. Applying that idea to complete aircraft raises a further question: how can a face classifier support geometric processing when the target regions have different representations and the processing itself changes the graph?

Three aspects of this question guide our design. First, a face's size or surface type can be ambiguous without its attachment to neighbouring faces. We combine face attributes with shared-boundary relations and model-level context. Second, the two target categories are sparse relative to background, and a background face misidentified as a target becomes a candidate for an unintended edit. We formulate a mutually exclusive face partition and use class-weighted supervision with explicit background-error costs. Third, labels refer to a particular B-Rep state. We rebuild the face graph after rivet processing and use geometric correspondence to carry unchanged candidates into the cosmetic-feature stage.

The resulting framework assigns every face to background, rivet, or cosmetic feature. The last category groups the annotated non-rivet targets, including window and decal regions, for subsequent local reconstruction. This task partition determines the categorical output; the geometric representation still determines the operation applied to a selected region. The method joins face-level learning to a staged editing workflow while keeping neural predictions, processed recognition labels, and CAD proposals identifiable throughout evaluation.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item A task formulation and attributed face-graph representation for rivet and cosmetic-feature recognition in complete aircraft B-Reps, with predictions linked to the corresponding editable faces.
  \item An integration of categorical face recognition with staged CAD processing, using graph re-extraction and geometric correspondence to connect predictions across topology changes.
  \item An aircraft case study that reports class-specific and per-aircraft recognition errors together with paired CAD and surface-mesh measurements under fixed tessellation controls.
\end{enumerate}

The experiments examine recognition errors, the transition from recognised faces to CAD operations, and the resulting surface-mesh scale. Training uses 17 aircraft; the nine additional aircraft used for evaluation also informed model and rule development. The findings consequently describe this developmental setting. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} identifies the independent evaluation and geometric measurements needed to extend them.

\section{Related work}\label{sec2}

\subsection{Learning semantic information from B-Reps}

The choice of representation determines how a recognition result can be used in CAD. PointNet learns shape and point features from unordered samples \parencite{qi2017pointnet}; a CAD operation additionally requires a correspondence between the predicted region and the source faces. B-Rep learning exposes this correspondence directly. UV-Net combines samples from parametric domains with adjacency, whereas BRepNet defines convolutional neighbourhoods through oriented coedges \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet}. These methods show how geometric evidence and topological incidence can support face-level prediction.

Machining-feature recognition adds application-specific semantics to these representations. Hierarchical CADNet combines face and mesh levels to describe surface geometry and topology \parencite{colligan2022hierarchical}. AAGNet uses geometric attributed adjacency graphs for semantic, instance, and bottom-face segmentation \parencite{wu2024aagnet}. The present task instead labels rivet and cosmetic-feature faces on complete aircraft and uses those labels to construct editing candidates. These face-based representations motivate our use of adjacency for aircraft target recognition.

\subsection{Feature recognition and geometric editing}

Classical feature-recognition methods encode geometric and topological patterns through graph matching, decomposition, and hint-based reasoning \parencite{han2000manufacturing,shah2001discourse,babic2008review}. Explicit patterns make a recognised feature interpretable, while variations in face partitioning and interacting features complicate matching. Learned face representations provide another source of semantic evidence, but the transition from a face label to an editing operation still depends on the local CAD structure.

This distinction is particularly relevant to windows and decals. An embedded patch and a split skin region can share a semantic label while requiring different reconstruction procedures. Our classifier supplies face categories, and configured CAD operators act on those categories using the current geometry. Re-extraction and correspondence address the change in face enumeration between operations. These steps connect recognition to editing without treating a predicted face count as a count of feature instances.

\subsection{CAD simplification and surface meshing}

CAD simplification changes geometric entities before discretisation. Thakur et al. organise simulation-oriented simplification by the entities and operators involved \parencite{thakur2009cad_simplification}. Analysis-aware defeaturing further evaluates the effect of removing geometry on a specified differential problem; Antol\'{i}n and Chanon develop error estimation for Neumann features \parencite{antolin2024analysis_aware_defeaturing}. Such analysis requires physical and boundary-condition information beyond a semantic face label. We use the labels to select the target geometry and measure the resulting surface meshes.

Mesh simplification acts on a different representation. Quadric error metrics (QEM) reduce a polygonal surface through vertex contractions \parencite{garland1997qem}, whereas B-Rep processing changes the surfaces and trimming boundaries supplied to a mesher. Tessellation studies also treat the handling of narrow and curved regions as a geometric problem \parencite{li2026robust_tessellation}. We therefore compare original and processed B-Reps under identical meshing controls, and use QEM at matched triangle counts as a reference for measured computational components. Geometric approximation error and downstream simulation accuracy form separate evaluation criteria.

\subsection{B-Rep topology and geometry}\label{sec:brep_preliminary}

A B-Rep combines geometric supports with topological incidence \parencite{iso10303_42_2025}. Vertices bound edges, ordered edges form wires, wires trim faces, and connected faces form shells. A face occupies a bounded domain of an analytic or spline surface; its trimming curves and any inner wires determine the represented region (Figure~\ref{fig:brep_preliminary}). Faces on similar supporting surfaces can have different boundaries and attachments. Recognition must therefore distinguish surface shape from the way a face is embedded in the surrounding model.

\subsection{Face-adjacency graphs}\label{sec:face_graph_preliminary}

We represent the B-Rep by a graph $G=(V,E)$ in which node $v_i$ corresponds to face $f_i$, and $(v_i,v_j)\in E$ records a shared topological boundary (Figure~\ref{fig:face_graph_preliminary}). Node vectors $\mathbf{x}_i$ describe individual faces and edge vectors $\mathbf{e}_{ij}$ describe their relations. A stored face identifier connects each graph node to its CAD entity. This identifier is local to the current B-Rep state: an editing operation can split, merge, remove, or reorder faces, so correspondence must be re-established after that operation.

\subsection{Message passing on attributed graphs}\label{sec:message_passing_preliminary}

Message passing combines information from adjacent faces while preserving a representation for each node. A general layer takes the form

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_i^{(\ell)}=
\operatorname{AGG}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}
\Phi^{(\ell)}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell)},\mathbf{h}_j^{(\ell)},\mathbf{e}_{ij}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell+1)}=
\Psi^{(\ell)}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell)},\mathbf{m}_i^{(\ell)}\right),
\label{eq:generic_message_passing}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}(i)$ is the neighbourhood of node $i$, $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ constructs a message, and $\Psi^{(\ell)}$ updates the node state using a permutation-invariant aggregation. In edge-conditioned convolution, the relation attributes determine the message filter \parencite{simonovsky2017dynamic}. Graph pooling supplies a complementary summary of the complete aircraft. Section~\ref{sec:three_class_learning} specifies how the model combines this global summary with each face's local representation.

\section{Face-level graph learning}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Supervised face-level task}\label{sec:data_preparation}

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the faces of an aircraft B-Rep and $y_i\in\{0,1,2\}$ the annotation of face $f_i$. Label 1 denotes a face annotated as part of a rivet. Label 2 denotes an annotated non-rivet target, termed a \textit{cosmetic feature}, including window and decal regions. Label 0, \textit{background}, is the complement of these annotated target sets. These labels define the target selection task; background is not a universal statement about a face's structural role, and cosmetic feature does not imply irrelevance to every engineering analysis.

The three-class formulation expresses the required partition directly. It distinguishes the two target categories used by the editing workflow and retains an explicit class for the surrounding geometry. Windows and decals share the non-rivet target label, while their geometric representation determines the reconstruction operation. The stored annotations assign exactly one label to each face. The classifier predicts this face partition rather than feature instances or editing operators.

For each face, the output consists of a probability vector and a categorical label linked to its face identifier. We use $b$, $r$, and $s$ for background, rivet, and cosmetic feature in equations, corresponding to stored labels 0, 1, and 2. The implementation field \texttt{surface\_feature} denotes label 2 throughout. Recognition is evaluated on the original B-Rep; subsequent predictions refer to the re-extracted geometry in Section~\ref{sec:geometry_protocol}.

The node vector combines scale and shape, surface type, boundary organisation, attachment relations, normal similarity, and model-relative position. \subsection{Network architecture and training}\label{sec:three_class_learning}

\subsubsection{Local and global face encoding}\label{sec:three_class_gnn}

The network combines local geometric relations with context from the complete aircraft. Four edge-conditioned convolution layers propagate information along shared boundaries; mean and max pooling summarise the resulting face states. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) then maps the local and pooled features to a categorical distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:three_class_gnn}). The network has 318,217 parameters.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\input{figures/fig_three_class_gnn_architecture.tex}
\caption{Face classification from local geometry and graph context. (A) B-Rep graph with 48 node and six edge attributes. (B) Four NNConv layers of width 64, followed by local/global concatenation. (C) An MLP with a 192-dimensional hidden layer and three logits. Face identifiers link the categorical output to CAD processing.}\label{fig:three_class_gnn}
\end{figure}

The raw node and edge attributes are transformed by a node-input encoder and an edge-input encoder:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(0)}=\phi_x(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}=\phi_e(\mathbf{e}_{ij}).
\label{eq:shared_input_encoding}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{48}$ and $\mathbf{e}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}$. Each input encoder applies a linear transformation, LayerNorm, and ReLU while preserving its input width.

Each NNConv layer uses an edge MLP, $6\rightarrow16\rightarrow d_{\mathrm{in}}d_{\mathrm{out}}$ with an intermediate ReLU, to generate the neighbour transformation \parencite{simonovsky2017dynamic}. Messages are summed and combined with the layer's learned root transformation and bias. LayerNorm, ReLU, and dropout then give

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(\ell+1)}=
\operatorname{Dropout}\!\left(\operatorname{ReLU}\!\left(\operatorname{LN}\!\left(
\operatorname{NNConv}_{\ell}(\{\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(\ell)}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)\cup\{i\}},\{\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)})
\right)\right)\right).
\label{eq:shared_message_update}
\end{equation}
The first layer maps 48 features to 64. Layers 2--4 retain width 64 and add the preceding node state:
\[
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell+1)}=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}, & \ell=0,\\
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(\ell+1)}+\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}, & \ell=1,2,3.
\end{cases}
\]
After the fourth layer, mean and max pooling over the aircraft's face nodes $V$ each yield a 64-dimensional vector. Appending their concatenation to the local node state gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{c}&=\left[\operatorname{Mean}_{j\in V}\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(4)}
\,\Vert\,\operatorname{Max}_{j\in V}\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(4)}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{128},\\
\mathbf{z}_i&=\left[\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(4)}\,\Vert\,\mathbf{c}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{192}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:local_global_context}
\end{equation}
An MLP maps $\mathbf{z}_i$ to a 192-dimensional hidden state using LayerNorm, ReLU, and dropout, followed by a linear map to three logits $\mathbf{a}_i$. The categorical probabilities are

\begin{equation}
p_{i,k}=\frac{\exp(a_{i,k})}{\sum_{j\in\{b,r,s\}}\exp(a_{i,j})},
\qquad k\in\{b,r,s\},
\label{eq:three_class_probabilities}
\end{equation}
where $b$, $r$, and $s$ denote background, rivet, and cosmetic feature. The probabilities sum to one, expressing competition among the three face categories.

\subsubsection{Training objective and inference rule}\label{sec:training_inference}

Background dominates the training faces. We use class weights to change the relative contribution of each category and add costs for predicting target labels on background examples. Class imbalance motivates this training design \parencite{buda2018imbalance}; the particular weights are part of our implementation. For training-set class counts $n_k$ and $N_{\mathrm{tr}}=\sum_k n_k$, let $\tilde{w}_k=\min\{5,\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{tr}}/(3n_k)}\}$ and $w_k=3\tilde{w}_k/\sum_j\tilde{w}_j$. For a minibatch of $N$ supervised faces,

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[&-w_{y_i}\log p_{i,y_i}
-3\,\mathbf{1}_{y_i=b}\log(1-p_{i,r})\\
&-2\,\mathbf{1}_{i\in H}\log(1-p_{i,s})\big].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:categorical_supervision}
\end{equation}
The set $H$ contains true background faces with either normalised area at least 0.01 and shared-boundary ratio at most 0.2, or a smooth connected component with normalised area at least 0.25 and at least two faces. Here, normalised area is $A_i(2/L)^2$, where $A_i$ is face area and $L$ is the longest extent of the aircraft bounding box; component area is the sum over its faces. A smooth component joins adjacent faces of the same surface type across smooth edges with absolute mean dihedral angle at most 0.05~rad. The shared-boundary ratio is total shared-boundary length divided by face perimeter, capped at one. Probabilities in the added cost terms are clipped below one for numerical stability. The masks use the training labels to restrict the added costs to background examples.

Soft auxiliary targets provide an additional training signal through knowledge distillation \parencite{hinton2015distilling}. A pretrained teacher supplied rivet and cosmetic-feature logits $\ell_{i,r}$ and $\ell_{i,s}$ on the 17 training aircraft. The cached categorical targets use

\[
\mathbf{t}_i=\left[0,\ \ell_{i,r}-\operatorname{logit}(0.9),\
\ell_{i,s}-\operatorname{logit}(0.5)\right].
\]
The offsets define the cached target distribution. With $q_i^{(T)}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{t}_i/T)$ and $p_i^{(T)}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{a}_i/T)$, the total objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}+
\frac{T^2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{KL}\!\left(q_i^{(T)}\,\Vert\,p_i^{(T)}\right),
\qquad T=2.
\label{eq:distilled_categorical_objective}
\end{equation}
The retained cache contains the training aircraft only, aligned by face identifiers and truth hashes. The encoder initialisation and cached logits are retained with the training assets. Their use fixes the supervision for this run without requiring a teacher during inference.

At inference, the network is evaluated at temperature one and assigns the most probable category:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}^{\mathrm{raw}}_i=\underset{k\in\{b,r,s\}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\ p_{i,k}.
\label{eq:categorical_decision}
\end{equation}
The raw label, probability vector, and face identifier are exported together. Geometric candidate processing produces a separate system label $\hat{y}^{\mathrm{sys}}_i$ (Section~\ref{sec:risk_filtering}). A recognition pass predicts the complete face partition; the order of later CAD operations follows the topology-dependent workflow below.

\section{CAD processing and experimental setup}\label{sec:processing_pipeline}

\subsection{From face labels to candidates}\label{sec:risk_filtering}

The geometric candidate stage converts raw classifications into the system labels used for recognition evaluation. It completes eligible window patches, rejects oversized rivet candidates and characteristic wing/shell patches, screens planar rivet patches and fully occluded cosmetic candidates, and applies a confidence gate when cosmetic predictions occupy at least half of a model. The sequence and fixed parameters are given in Appendix~\ref{sec:candidate_parameters}. Neural probabilities remain attached to their faces; geometric completion does not recalibrate them.

Window completion uses host inner wires, smooth adjacency, bilateral correspondence, and bounded trim fragments. Its retained profile is restricted by file hash to the recorded DC-10 geometry and was developed on that model. The remaining screens use the current geometry and prediction distribution. All steps are included in $\hat{y}^{\mathrm{sys}}$ and in the system metrics. Section~\ref{sec:e1_results} also reports raw network scores to identify the role of this processing.

\subsection{Staged removal and reconstruction}\label{sec:geometry_protocol}

CAD processing uses the face references associated with the current B-Rep. Rivet candidates are processed first because those operations change the surface partition on which subsequent recognition operates. We then extract the intermediate faces, attributes, and adjacency and run the same trained classifier on the rebuilt graph. Cosmetic-feature processing acts on this intermediate geometry.

To retain unchanged patches that receive different predictions after re-extraction, we match original cosmetic candidates to current faces using surface type, centre, area, and perimeter. Additional proposals identify repeated small panes and smoothly attached decorative patches. Large perforated carrier faces are retained as reconstruction hosts. These proposals form the CAD candidate set $\mathcal{C}_1$, with size $C_1=|\mathcal{C}_1|$; their geometric provenance is stored separately from the recognition labels. Appendix~\ref{sec:cad_parameters} specifies the correspondence and proposal criteria.

The workflow is thus: extract the original graph and classify its faces; process rivet candidates; rebuild and classify the intermediate graph; establish geometric correspondence and complete CAD proposals; then apply the cosmetic-feature operator. Reusing the trained model gives the two passes the same class semantics, while rebuilding the graph gives each pass the appropriate geometry and face references.

Figure~\ref{fig:geometry_protocol} shows the current G280 sequence with fixed cameras. The rivet stage removes selected wing details. Cosmetic-feature processing then reconstructs the window hosts and tail-lettering regions using the intermediate geometry. The insets compare the same spatial regions across the stages.

The experiment uses four configured operation families: predicted-face removal, inner-wire host reconstruction for embedded panes, split-skin reconstruction and bridging, and local host reconstruction. \subsection{Data annotation and training protocol}\label{sec:data_training}

\subsubsection{Annotation contract and model-level partitioning}\label{sec:data_partition}

The task recognises native B-Rep entities: faces and their shared topological boundaries in prepared aircraft STEP models. The dataset combines source-model annotations with procedurally constructed rivet and decorative geometry. After STEP export and reload, face records are collapsed to background (0), rivet (1), and cosmetic feature (2). The experiments use the frozen face labels stored with the model bundle, aligned with predictions by aircraft and face identifier. All recognition measurements refer to the prepared inputs before removal.

All faces of a given aircraft file remain in one partition, preserving the grouping of local adjacency relations \parencite{kapoor2023leakage}. Training contains 17 aircraft and evaluation contains nine additional aircraft (Table~\ref{tab:data_composition}). Normalisation statistics, supervised updates, and cached auxiliary logits use the training partition. The nine-aircraft set informed architecture selection and geometric-rule development, including the DC-10 correction. We refer to it as a developmental evaluation set throughout; it measures behaviour on these known aircraft.

\subsubsection{Training configuration and reproducibility}\label{sec:training_configuration}

Each aircraft is trained as a complete graph. The retained bundle contains weights, feature order, training-set normalisation, class mapping, configuration, training history, encoder initialisation, and training-only auxiliary logits. Inference loads the paired weights and statistics.

The network uses four width-64 convolution layers, a 192-dimensional MLP hidden layer, and dropout 0.1. Initialisation uses the retained pretrained encoder. It remains frozen in evaluation mode for 40 epochs, followed by 160 epochs of joint fine-tuning. AdamW uses base learning rates of $2\times10^{-5}$ for the encoder and $10^{-3}$ for the head, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and gradient-norm clipping at 2 \parencite{loshchilov2019adamw}. At epoch $e$, both rates use the multiplier $0.1+0.45[1+\cos(\pi(e-1)/199)]$; the encoder rate is zero while frozen. The effective batch contains eight graphs. One graph at a time contributes gradients weighted by its supervised-face count, followed by a batch update. The run uses seed 123 and the fixed final epoch 200. Model configuration, rather than the final-epoch rule, was selected using the known evaluation results.

\section{Experimental results}\label{sec:results}

The experiments address three questions: how well the system identifies each target category; how predictions become candidates for staged CAD editing; and how the processed geometry changes the surface-mesh size and mesh-generation time. Recognition uses frozen truth on the original B-Reps. CAD and mesh measurements use the recorded intermediate and final endpoints from the same trained model. Input views colour the original annotations, and processed views use uniform shading.

The execution environment is Windows 11, Intel Core i7-14700KF, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 (8~GB), Python 3.10.20, and PyTorch 2.12.1+cu130. All reported values are from the retained run and paired endpoint measurements.

\subsection{Recognition accuracy and error distribution}\label{sec:e1_results}

We evaluate every face in the nine-aircraft developmental set, giving 34,162 prediction--truth pairs. Precision measures the fraction of a class's predicted faces that are correct; recall measures the fraction of its true faces recovered. Their harmonic mean is $F1=2PR/(P+R)$, and intersection over union is $\mathrm{IoU}=TP/(TP+FP+FN)$. Counts are pooled over faces for the aggregate scores, with per-aircraft results reported separately because the models differ greatly in size.

Table~\ref{tab:e1_class_metrics} reports the complete recognition system $\hat{y}^{\mathrm{sys}}$. Before geometric processing, raw argmax predictions give rivet F1 0.1895 and cosmetic-feature F1 0.6505, with 3,149 background faces assigned to targets. The system gives F1 0.9297 and 0.7776, respectively, and 209 background false positives. For rivets, raw prediction recovers 339 of 346 true faces but also selects 2,892 background faces; the system retains 311 true rivet faces and 12 background false positives. The change reflects the combined geometric processing, including its rejection of some true targets. These scores characterise the implemented system rather than isolate the contribution of the network architecture.

Loading, feature extraction, recognition, CAD editing, and mesh-statistic extraction lie outside this timer. \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The experiments establish a traceable route from face categories to staged CAD endpoints and their surface meshes. The face graph preserves the entities needed for editing, and re-extraction with geometric correspondence connects the classifier to a changed face partition. The three-class output supplies the required semantic partition, while operation selection remains configured from each model's representation. This division explains why joint classification and sequential geometry processing serve different roles in the workflow.

Recognition quality is uneven across aircraft and depends materially on geometric candidate processing. The raw and system scores quantify this dependence, but they do not separate the effects of individual rules, feature groups, pooled context, background costs, or distillation. The current single-seed run and known-model development set support a case study; controlled ablations, repeated training, and independent aircraft are required for conclusions about architectural advantage and transfer. The data include generated target geometry, so transfer to naturally occurring rivet representations also requires evaluation. In particular, the retained DC-10 completion profile is specific to the evaluated geometry.

The labels express the annotated editing targets, not their importance to a specified physical problem. The CAD examples demonstrate recorded processing outcomes, and the mesh experiment measures surface-triangle counts and mesher time. Quantified geometric deviation, removal completeness, mesh quality at matched approximation error, downstream simulation response, and total workflow cost remain requirements for a broader comparison. Analysis-aware assessment would require loads, boundary conditions, and accuracy criteria appropriate to the intended application \parencite{antolin2024analysis_aware_defeaturing}.

\section{Conclusions}

We presented an attributed face graph framework for recognising rivet and cosmetic-feature faces in aircraft B-Reps and connecting those predictions to staged geometric processing. Local boundary relations and pooled aircraft context support categorical face prediction; graph re-extraction and geometric correspondence maintain the connection to editable faces after rivet processing.

On nine developmental evaluation aircraft, the complete recognition system achieves rivet F1 0.9297 and cosmetic-feature F1 0.7776, with errors concentrated in particular models. The resulting CAD endpoints produce fewer surface triangles and shorter median mesh-generation times in all nine paired measurements under fixed controls. The study provides an auditable basis for extending face-based editing to independent aircraft and for evaluating the geometric and analysis-specific consequences of those edits.

\section{Candidate-processing parameters}\label{sec:candidate_parameters}

The following settings specify the fixed chain that produces the system labels. They do not modify the neural probability vector.

\paragraph{Window completion.}
The DC-10 profile is enabled only for the recorded STEP hash. It requires at least eight B-spline pane seeds bounded by host inner wires, with smooth adjacencies within 0.05~rad and seed area below one quarter of host area. A mirrored patch must have an area ratio at most 1.1, reflected-centre distance at most $0.1\sqrt{A}$ for patch area $A$, area between 0.1 and 10 times the median seed area, and compactness at most 3. Connected eligible patches touching a seed or host are included. A smooth B-spline trim fragment is included when its area is below 0.1 times the median seed area, compactness exceeds 3, and its two neighbours are one selected pane and one host.

\paragraph{Suppression screens.}
The wing/shell screen rejects a cosmetic-labelled B-spline face with no inner wire when either of two patterns holds: (i) four edges, relative area at least 0.0013, compactness at least 1.8, four planar neighbours, no curved neighbour, four convex edges, and no smooth edge; or (ii) five edges, relative area in $[0.0004,0.001]$, compactness in $[1.4,1.6]$, at least four planar neighbours, at most one curved neighbour, at least four convex edges, and at most one smooth edge. Here, relative area is face area divided by total B-Rep face area. A rivet-size screen rejects relative area above $5\times10^{-6}$. A planar rivet screen rejects a planar face with at least four planar neighbours and no smooth edge. Rejected labels become background.

\paragraph{Visibility and density.}
A cosmetic candidate is rejected if sampled points have no unblocked exposure along any of the six Cartesian directions. An intersector exception leaves exposure unknown and retains the candidate for subsequent processing. Finally, if cosmetic candidates occupy at least half of the faces, those with neural cosmetic probability below 0.7 become background. The order is window completion, wing/shell and rivet-size screens, planar rivet screen, visibility screen, and density screen.

\section{Correspondence and CAD proposals}\label{sec:cad_parameters}

For original-to-intermediate correspondence, matched faces must have the same surface type. Centre distance is at most $\max(10^{-5}D,10^{-5})$, where $D$ is the largest coordinate extent of current face centres, bounded below by 1. Area difference is at most $\max(10^{-4}A,10^{-8})$, and perimeter difference at most $\max(5\times10^{-4}P,10^{-7})$, using original area $A$ and perimeter $P$. These tests locate unchanged geometry without assuming persistent face numbering.

Additional CAD proposals require neural cosmetic probability at least 0.1, relative area at most 0.001, and a largest neighbour at least four times the face area. Repeated-pane proposals require a B-spline surface, two edges, at least one smooth edge, no inner wire, and at least eight similarly sized faces in a group whose maximum-to-minimum area ratio is at most 1.25. Single-host decorative patches require a B-spline surface, at least six edges, one wire, exactly one smooth edge, one distinct neighbour, and minimum neighbouring-normal dot product at least 0.99. Rivet-labelled faces are excluded from these additions. The transferred candidate set protects carrier faces with at least eight inner wires and relative area at least 0.01. The resulting proposals are used for CAD processing and are not relabelled truth for the original recognition experiment.
