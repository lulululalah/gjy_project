\begin{abstract}
Geometric feature recognition in boundary representation (B-Rep) models supports computer-aided design (CAD) editing and analysis and has been widely studied for fillets, chamfers, and holes. In preparing aircraft models for numerical simulation, small geometric details can require local mesh refinement, increasing surface-triangle counts and mesh-generation time. We present a face-level graph-learning framework for recognising these details and guiding targeted CAD simplification. The task distinguishes rivets, cosmetic features, and background; cosmetic features denote annotated non-rivet targets, including window and decal regions. Each B-Rep face forms a graph node, and shared boundaries define adjacency. An edge-conditioned graph neural network combines local geometric and topological attributes with aircraft-level context to predict a category for each face. Predictions retain their correspondence with the source geometry and guide staged feature removal and local surface reconstruction. The framework is evaluated on a test set containing 34,162 faces. Across nine aircraft models and under fixed meshing settings, processing reduced the surface-triangle count by a mean of 20.88\% and the median mesh-generation time by a mean of 36.29\% relative to the original models. By connecting feature recognition with editable geometry, the framework supports targeted model idealisation and reduces the surface-meshing workload in numerical simulation preparation.
\end{abstract}

{\small\textbf{Keywords}: B-Rep feature recognition, graph learning, rivet recognition, cosmetic features, geometric processing.}

\section{Introduction}

Design-complete CAD models and downstream numerical simulation are developed under different and often poorly connected requirements. Designers model the product as completely as possible, retaining local details such as rivets, fasteners, window and decal regions, as well as internal structures, because these entities carry design intent, manufacturing definition, or assembly information. For simulation, however, many of these entities are not merely unnecessary but harmful: small faces and local details force local mesh refinement, multiply surface triangles, prolong mesh generation, and can degrade element quality, while internal or non-structural components may introduce geometric partitions that are irrelevant to the analysis. The gap between a design-complete CAD model and a simulation-ready model is therefore not simply a matter of degree but of representation: the entities that carry design information are often exactly the entities that should not be resolved by the downstream mesher. Identifying which geometric units are irrelevant to a given simulation purpose, and removing or reconstructing them without damaging the editable CAD model, is thus a critical step in simulation preparation.

In complete aircraft B-Rep models, these details are not stored as semantic labels but as ordinary faces and shared boundaries. A rivet may be represented by several small faces, a window by an embedded patch or a partition of the surrounding skin, and a decal by a trimmed surface region. Identifying these structures is therefore a problem of interpreting both surface geometry and its topological context, and the output must locate editable B-Rep faces, since a semantic region alone does not specify the entities on which removal or reconstruction should act. Figure~\ref{fig:gulfstream_target_examples} illustrates the target regions considered here.

CAD feature recognition has long used attributed adjacency graphs, geometric reasoning, and decomposition to recover manufacturing semantics \parencite{han2000manufacturing,shah2001discourse,babic2008review}. Learning directly from B-Rep entities extends this approach by combining geometry with topology, as demonstrated by UV-Net, BRepNet, Hierarchical CADNet, and AAGNet \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet,colligan2022hierarchical,wu2024aagnet}. Their representations provide a basis for face prediction. Applying that idea to complete aircraft raises a further question: how can a face classifier support geometric processing when the target regions have different representations and the processing itself changes the graph?

Three aspects of this question guide our design. First, a face's size or surface type can be ambiguous without its attachment to neighbouring faces. We combine face attributes with shared-boundary relations and model-level context. Second, the two target categories are sparse relative to background, and a background face misidentified as a target becomes a candidate for an unintended edit. We formulate a mutually exclusive face partition and use class-weighted supervision with explicit background-error costs. Third, labels refer to a particular B-Rep state. We rebuild the face graph after rivet processing and use geometric correspondence to carry unchanged candidates into the cosmetic-feature stage.

The resulting framework assigns every face to background, rivet, or cosmetic feature. The last category groups the annotated non-rivet targets, including window and decal regions, for subsequent local reconstruction. This task partition determines the categorical output; the geometric representation still determines the operation applied to a selected region. The method joins face-level learning to a staged editing workflow while keeping neural predictions, processed recognition labels, and CAD proposals identifiable throughout evaluation.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item A task formulation and attributed face-graph representation for rivet and cosmetic-feature recognition in complete aircraft B-Reps, with predictions linked to the corresponding editable faces.
  \item An integration of categorical face recognition with staged CAD processing, using graph re-extraction and geometric correspondence to connect predictions across topology changes.
  \item Paired comparisons of original and processed aircraft models under fixed meshing settings show reductions in surface-triangle count and median mesh-generation time, connecting targeted CAD simplification to mesh preparation for numerical simulation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec2}

For cosmetic-feature processing in complete aircraft B-Rep models, relevant studies mainly arise from two directions: three-dimensional scene segmentation and CAD feature recognition. The former focuses on locating semantic or instance regions in sampled 3D data, whereas the latter concerns recognising, within editable models, entities that can support subsequent geometric operations. For industrial CAD models, obtaining only region-level recognition results is insufficient to directly support feature removal or reconstruction; the results must also correspond to parametric faces and their topological relations. The following review therefore considers three-dimensional scene segmentation, CAD feature recognition, and the requirements of small-geometry recognition for downstream simulation processing.

\subsection{Three-dimensional deep learning and scene segmentation}

Three-dimensional deep learning has developed several representations for semantic and instance segmentation, including voxels, point clouds, polygon meshes, and multi-view images. PointNet established a direct route to point-level segmentation by learning from unordered point sets \parencite{qi2017pointnet}. Later open-vocabulary methods incorporated image evidence into reconstructed 3D scenes. OpenMask3D fuses features from multiple views for each 3D instance proposal, whereas Open3DIS lifts two-dimensional instance masks to point-cloud regions and merges proposals across views \parencite{takmaz2023openmask3d,nguyen2024open3dis}. Together, these approaches illustrate how image features and multi-view projection can extend recognition beyond a fixed set of training labels.

The output of OpenMask3D and Open3DIS is a semantic or instance region derived from reconstructed point clouds and posed RGB-D images, typically in indoor or otherwise reconstructed scenes \parencite{takmaz2023openmask3d,nguyen2024open3dis}. Such a region is not, by itself, a reference to the parametric faces or topological relations of an editable CAD solid. Consequently, using a scene-segmentation result for local geometry removal would require an additional mapping from the detected region back to the B-Rep. CAD feature-recognition methods are designed around this entity-level requirement and provide a more direct starting point for editable-model operations.

\subsection{CAD feature recognition from rules to B-Rep learning}

CAD feature-recognition research has traditionally focused on extracting manufacturing information and supporting computer-aided process planning. Within this line of work, geometric rules, attributed adjacency graphs, volumetric decomposition and recomposition, and feature hints have been used to identify local structures such as holes, slots, and bosses \parencite{han2000manufacturing,shah2001discourse,babic2008review}. Their main advantage is interpretability when feature definitions and topological templates are explicit. Their transfer becomes more difficult, however, when features interact, thresholds are domain specific, surfaces are free-form, or the same design intent is represented by different face partitions \parencite{shah2001discourse}. Attributed adjacency graphs offer a natural bridge to learning-based recognition because they preserve faces together with their topological relationships. However, conventional subgraph matching remains tied to predefined patterns, which limits its adaptability when topology varies or features interact.

Recent CAD-learning methods retain B-Rep entities and their relations as part of the learning representation, thereby moving beyond recognition based solely on hand-crafted templates. UV-Net combines parametric surface and curve information with adjacency-graph topology, while BRepNet performs message passing over faces, edges, and oriented coedges for modelling-operation segmentation \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet}. Hierarchical CADNet augments a B-Rep face-adjacency graph with a surface-mesh level and edge convexity for machining-feature recognition, and AAGNet investigates multi-task machining-feature recognition on attributed adjacency graphs \parencite{colligan2022hierarchical,wu2024aagnet}. These studies establish several effective ways to expose B-Rep geometry and topology to face-level learning. The problem setting remains different from the one considered here: their datasets and labels focus mainly on mechanical parts, synthetic CAD collections, modelling-operation semantics, or machining features, rather than distinguishing \textit{background} faces, \textit{rivet} faces, and diverse \textit{cosmetic feature} faces in complete-aircraft models.

\subsection{Gap for small geometry in industrial simulation workflows}

At the meshing stage, \textcite{li2026robust_tessellation} use critical regions and constrained Delaunay triangulation to produce watertight B-Rep tessellations without self-intersections, including narrow and highly curved regions. Their work addresses the robustness of discretisation and complements approaches that simplify the input geometry before mesh generation.

In industrial simulation workflows, recognising a local feature is only an intermediate step: the result may determine whether that feature is retained, removed, or reconstructed before meshing and analysis. CAD simplification and analysis-aware defeaturing have examined the geometric consequences of feature removal \parencite{thakur2009cad_simplification,antolin2024analysis_aware_defeaturing}, and B-Rep learning has shown that face-based predictions can remain linked to their source entities \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet,yeo2021machining}. These two strands address complementary requirements, but the literature provides limited documentation of a route that identifies small, heterogeneous local geometry on complete industrial models and carries face-level predictions into CAD processing.

Aggregate segmentation accuracy does not indicate which background faces may be modified incorrectly. A false positive on a large smooth shell face, for example, may be more consequential to subsequent geometry processing than a missed local target. This motivates a B-Rep face-level formulation in which predictions can be located on the source model and errors are reported for each aircraft. In the representation developed next, faces serve as graph nodes and shared boundaries provide local context.

\subsection{B-Rep topology and geometry}\label{sec:brep_preliminary}

A B-Rep combines geometric supports with topological incidence \parencite{iso10303_42_2025}. Vertices bound edges, ordered edges form wires, wires trim faces, and connected faces form shells. A face occupies a bounded domain of an analytic or spline surface; its trimming curves and any inner wires determine the represented region (Figure~\ref{fig:brep_preliminary}). Faces on similar supporting surfaces can have different boundaries and attachments. Recognition must therefore distinguish surface shape from the way a face is embedded in the surrounding model.

\subsection{Face-adjacency graphs}\label{sec:face_graph_preliminary}

We represent the B-Rep by a graph $G=(V,E)$ in which node $v_i$ corresponds to face $f_i$, and $(v_i,v_j)\in E$ records a shared topological boundary (Figure~\ref{fig:face_graph_preliminary}). Node vectors $\mathbf{x}_i$ describe individual faces and edge vectors $\mathbf{e}_{ij}$ describe their relations. A stored face identifier connects each graph node to its CAD entity. This identifier is local to the current B-Rep state: an editing operation can split, merge, remove, or reorder faces, so correspondence must be re-established after that operation.

\subsection{Message passing on attributed graphs}\label{sec:message_passing_preliminary}

Message passing combines information from adjacent faces while preserving a representation for each node. A general layer takes the form

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_i^{(\ell)}=
\operatorname{AGG}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}
\Phi^{(\ell)}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell)},\mathbf{h}_j^{(\ell)},\mathbf{e}_{ij}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell+1)}=
\Psi^{(\ell)}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell)},\mathbf{m}_i^{(\ell)}\right),
\label{eq:generic_message_passing}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}(i)$ is the neighbourhood of node $i$, $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ constructs a message, and $\Psi^{(\ell)}$ updates the node state using a permutation-invariant aggregation. In edge-conditioned convolution, the relation attributes determine the message filter \parencite{simonovsky2017dynamic}. Graph pooling supplies a complementary summary of the complete aircraft. Section~\ref{sec:three_class_learning} specifies how the model combines this global summary with each face's local representation.

\section{Face-level graph learning}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Supervised face-level task}\label{sec:data_preparation}

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the faces of an aircraft B-Rep and $y_i\in\{0,1,2\}$ the annotation of face $f_i$. Label 1 denotes a face annotated as part of a rivet. Label 2 denotes an annotated non-rivet target, termed a \textit{cosmetic feature}, including window and decal regions. Label 0, \textit{background}, is the complement of these annotated target sets. These labels define the target selection task; background is not a universal statement about a face's structural role, and cosmetic feature does not imply irrelevance to every engineering analysis.

The three-class formulation expresses the required partition directly. It distinguishes the two target categories used by the editing workflow and retains an explicit class for the surrounding geometry. Windows and decals share the non-rivet target label, while their geometric representation determines the reconstruction operation. The stored annotations assign exactly one label to each face. The classifier predicts this face partition rather than feature instances or editing operators.

For each face, the output consists of a probability vector and a categorical label linked to its face identifier. We use $b$, $r$, and $s$ for background, rivet, and cosmetic feature in equations, corresponding to stored labels 0, 1, and 2. Recognition is evaluated in the original face domain; predictions on re-extracted geometry are related to this domain through the correspondence in Section~\ref{sec:geometry_protocol}.

\subsubsection{Local and global face encoding}\label{sec:three_class_gnn}

The network combines local geometric relations with context from the complete aircraft. Four edge-conditioned convolution layers propagate information along shared boundaries; mean and max pooling summarise the resulting face states. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) maps these features to three unnormalised class scores, or logits. The softmax function in Equation~\ref{eq:three_class_probabilities} converts the scores into non-negative class probabilities that sum to one. The argmax operation selects the category with the largest probability as the face label (Figure~\ref{fig:three_class_gnn}). The network has 318,217 parameters.

\centering
\makebox[\linewidth][c]{\input{figures/fig_three_class_gnn_architecture.tex}}
\caption{Face-level prediction on a B-Rep graph. (A) Faces are graph nodes and shared boundaries define edges. (B) A shared GNN produces local features; mean and max pooling provide global context, concatenated with each local feature. (C) An MLP and linear classifier assign face labels using softmax and argmax. Input colours link faces to nodes; output colours denote categories. Ellipses denote omitted units.}\label{fig:three_class_gnn}
\end{figure}

The raw node and edge attributes are transformed by a node-input encoder and an edge-input encoder:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(0)}=\phi_x(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}=\phi_e(\mathbf{e}_{ij}).
\label{eq:shared_input_encoding}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{48}$ and $\mathbf{e}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}$. Each input encoder applies a linear transformation, LayerNorm, and ReLU while preserving its input width.

Each NNConv layer uses an edge MLP, $6\rightarrow16\rightarrow d_{\mathrm{in}}d_{\mathrm{out}}$ with an intermediate ReLU, to generate the neighbour transformation \parencite{simonovsky2017dynamic}. Messages are summed and combined with the layer's learned root transformation and bias. LayerNorm, ReLU, and dropout then give

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(\ell+1)}=
\operatorname{Dropout}\!\left(\operatorname{ReLU}\!\left(\operatorname{LN}\!\left(
\operatorname{NNConv}_{\ell}(\{\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(\ell)}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)\cup\{i\}},\{\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)})
\right)\right)\right).
\label{eq:shared_message_update}
\end{equation}
The first layer maps 48 features to 64. Layers 2--4 retain width 64 and add the preceding node state:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell+1)}=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}, & \ell=0,\\
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(\ell+1)}+\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}, & \ell=1,2,3.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:residual_update}
\end{equation}
After the fourth layer, mean and max pooling over the aircraft's face nodes $V$ each yield a 64-dimensional vector. Appending their concatenation to the local node state gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{c}&=\left[\operatorname{Mean}_{j\in V}\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(4)}
\,\Vert\,\operatorname{Max}_{j\in V}\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(4)}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{128},\\
\mathbf{z}_i&=\left[\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(4)}\,\Vert\,\mathbf{c}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{192}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:local_global_context}
\end{equation}
An MLP maps $\mathbf{z}_i$ to a 192-dimensional hidden state using LayerNorm, ReLU, and dropout, followed by a linear map to three logits $\mathbf{a}_i$. Applying softmax gives the class probabilities

\begin{equation}
p_{i,k}=\frac{\exp(a_{i,k})}{\sum_{j\in\{b,r,s\}}\exp(a_{i,j})},
\qquad k\in\{b,r,s\},
\label{eq:three_class_probabilities}
\end{equation}
where $b$, $r$, and $s$ denote background, rivet, and cosmetic feature. The probabilities sum to one, expressing competition among the three face categories.

\subsubsection{Training objective and inference rule}\label{sec:training_inference}

Background dominates the training faces. We use class weights to change the relative contribution of each category and add costs for predicting target labels on background examples. Class imbalance motivates this training design \parencite{buda2018imbalance}; the particular weights are part of our implementation. For training-set class counts $n_k$ and $N_{\mathrm{tr}}=\sum_k n_k$, let $\tilde{w}_k=\min\{5,\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{tr}}/(3n_k)}\}$ and $w_k=3\tilde{w}_k/\sum_j\tilde{w}_j$. For a minibatch of $N$ supervised faces,

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[&-w_{y_i}\log p_{i,y_i}
-3\,\mathbf{1}_{y_i=b}\log(1-p_{i,r})\\
&-2\,\mathbf{1}_{i\in H}\log(1-p_{i,s})\big].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:categorical_supervision}
\end{equation}
The set $H$ contains true background faces with either normalised area at least 0.01 and shared-boundary ratio at most 0.2, or a smooth connected component with normalised area at least 0.25 and at least two faces. Here, normalised area is $A_i(2/L)^2$, where $A_i$ is face area and $L$ is the longest extent of the aircraft bounding box; component area is the sum over its faces. A smooth component joins adjacent faces of the same surface type across smooth edges with absolute mean dihedral angle at most 0.05~rad. The shared-boundary ratio is total shared-boundary length divided by face perimeter, capped at one. Probabilities in the added cost terms are clipped below one for numerical stability. The masks use the training labels to restrict the added costs to background examples.

Soft auxiliary targets provide an additional training signal through knowledge distillation \parencite{hinton2015distilling}. A pretrained teacher supplied rivet and cosmetic-feature logits $\ell_{i,r}$ and $\ell_{i,s}$ on the training partition. The cached categorical targets use

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{t}_i=\left[0,\ \ell_{i,r}-\operatorname{logit}(0.9),\
\ell_{i,s}-\operatorname{logit}(0.5)\right].
\label{eq:teacher_targets}
\end{equation}
The offsets define the cached target distribution. With $q_i^{(T)}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{t}_i/T)$ and $p_i^{(T)}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{a}_i/T)$, the total objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}+
\frac{T^2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{KL}\!\left(q_i^{(T)}\,\Vert\,p_i^{(T)}\right),
\qquad T=2.
\label{eq:distilled_categorical_objective}
\end{equation}
The retained cache contains the training aircraft only, aligned by face identifiers and truth hashes. The encoder initialisation and cached logits are retained with the training assets. Their use fixes the supervision for this run without requiring a teacher during inference.

At inference, the network is evaluated at temperature one and assigns the most probable category:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i=\underset{k\in\{b,r,s\}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\ p_{i,k}.
\label{eq:categorical_decision}
\end{equation}
The predicted label, probability vector, and face identifier are exported together. These face references connect the classification output to the geometric checks and CAD operations described below.

\section{CAD simplification and experimental setup}\label{sec:processing_pipeline}

\subsection{Geometric checks for CAD editing}\label{sec:risk_filtering}

Before CAD editing, the predicted rivet and cosmetic-feature faces undergo automatic geometric screening using predefined rules. The screening considers face size, boundary loops, neighbouring surfaces, and exterior visibility to exclude large supporting faces and characteristic wing or shell patches that locally resemble target features. For window candidates, the rules use inner boundary loops on host faces, smooth adjacency, and geometric symmetry to identify compatible panes for joint processing. When cosmetic predictions cover an unusually large proportion of the model's faces, those with low prediction confidence are suppressed.

The accepted candidate faces are then passed to the staged removal and reconstruction operations using their identifiers on the current B-Rep. A face is retained in the CAD model if its removal or reconstruction fails the B-Rep validity checks. The screening rules are stored with the model and processing scripts.

\subsection{Staged removal and reconstruction}\label{sec:geometry_protocol}

CAD processing uses the face references associated with the current B-Rep. Rivet candidates are processed first because those operations change the surface partition on which subsequent recognition operates. We then extract the intermediate faces, attributes, and adjacency and run the same trained classifier on the rebuilt graph. Cosmetic-feature processing acts on this intermediate geometry.

Faces that remain unchanged across the rivet stage are matched using surface type, centre, area, and perimeter. This correspondence carries face references to the intermediate B-Rep without assuming persistent face numbering. For recognition evaluation, the final rivet and cosmetic-feature selections are expressed in the original face domain. Surface and boundary geometry provide an additional correspondence check where integral descriptors differ after STEP reload. All other original faces retain the background label.

The workflow is thus: extract the original graph and classify its faces; process rivet candidates; rebuild and classify the intermediate graph; establish geometric correspondence; then process the cosmetic-feature regions. Reusing the trained model gives the two passes the same class semantics, while rebuilding the graph gives each pass the appropriate geometry and face references.

Figure~\ref{fig:geometry_protocol} shows the current G280 sequence with fixed cameras. The rivet stage removes selected wing details. Cosmetic-feature processing then reconstructs the window hosts and tail-lettering regions using the intermediate geometry. The insets compare the same spatial regions across the stages.

\subsubsection{Training configuration and reproducibility}\label{sec:training_configuration}

Each aircraft is trained as a complete graph. The retained bundle contains weights, feature order, training-set normalisation, class mapping, configuration, training history, encoder initialisation, and training-only auxiliary logits. Inference loads the paired weights and statistics.

The network uses four width-64 convolution layers, a 192-dimensional MLP hidden layer, and dropout 0.1. The pretrained encoder is fixed in evaluation mode during 40 epochs of head training, followed by 160 epochs of joint fine-tuning. AdamW uses base learning rates of $2\times10^{-5}$ for the encoder and $10^{-3}$ for the head, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and gradient-norm clipping at 2 \parencite{loshchilov2019adamw}. At epoch $e$, both rates use the multiplier $0.1+0.45[1+\cos(\pi(e-1)/199)]$. The effective batch contains eight graphs. One graph at a time contributes gradients weighted by its supervised-face count, followed by a batch update. The run uses seed 123 and the fixed final epoch 200.

Training and inference use the same node and edge attribute groups summarised in Table~\ref{tab:node_attribute_groups}. The feature order and training-set standardisation statistics are stored with the model.



\section{Experimental results}\label{sec:results}

The experiments address three questions: how well the system identifies each target category; how predictions become candidates for staged CAD editing; and how the processed geometry changes the surface-mesh size and mesh-generation time. Recognition compares the final face selections from Section~\ref{sec:geometry_protocol} with frozen truth on the original B-Reps. CAD and mesh measurements use the recorded intermediate and final endpoints from the same trained model. Input views highlight annotated target regions; processed views use uniform shading.

The execution environment is Windows 11, Intel Core i7-14700KF, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 (8~GB), Python 3.10.20, and PyTorch 2.12.1+cu130. All reported values are from the retained run and paired endpoint measurements.

\subsection{Recognition accuracy and error distribution}\label{sec:e1_results}

Recognition is evaluated on all 34,162 faces of the test set, with predictions and truth aligned by aircraft and face identifier. For each class, precision $P$ is the fraction of predicted faces that are correct, and recall $R$ is the fraction of true faces recovered. Let $TP$, $FP$, and $FN$ denote correctly predicted class faces, other faces incorrectly assigned to the class, and class faces missed by the prediction, respectively. The metrics are
\begin{equation}
P=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad R=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
F1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}.
\label{eq:recognition_metrics}
\end{equation}
Counts are pooled over faces for the full-test-set scores. Table~\ref{tab:e1_class_metrics} reports the same metrics within each aircraft, together with background-to-target false positives. Overall F1 is higher for rivets than for cosmetic features. The per-aircraft results show that missed targets and false selections remain concentrated in particular models, especially for cosmetic features.

\subsubsection{Original--simplified surface-mesh comparison}\label{sec:e3_results}

For each aircraft, both endpoints use the policy $\delta=5\times10^{-4}D$ and angular deflection 0.5~rad. The diagonal $D$ is recomputed for each endpoint, and the resulting $\delta$ is passed to OpenCASCADE as an absolute linear deflection, with parallel meshing enabled. Five runs each reload the endpoint and generate a new mesh; the reported time is the median duration of mesh generation. Loading, feature extraction, recognition, CAD editing, and mesh-statistic extraction lie outside this timer. Node counts sum the per-face triangulation nodes before coordinate stitching, so shared-boundary nodes can be counted in both incident faces. Stored source and weight hashes link measurements to the evaluated inputs.

Every final B-Rep produces fewer surface triangles under these controls (Table~\ref{tab:e3_mesh}). Across the nine paired models, the arithmetic mean reduction was 20.88\% in surface-triangle count and 36.29\% in median mesh-generation time. Individual triangle-count reductions ranged from 2.55\% for the 109 standard E-1 to 38.36\% for Gulfstream G280 v17; time reductions ranged from 1.09\% to 61.61\%. The differing magnitudes show that a reduction in geometric partitioning and a reduction in meshing time need not have the same proportion. The five-run medians describe the measured endpoints; no statistical significance test is applied.

The retained element-shape diagnostics also show that fewer triangles do not imply uniformly well-shaped elements. In the final meshes, the fifth percentile of minimum triangle angles ranges from $1.20^\circ$ to $3.07^\circ$. The angle statistic excludes triangles with Heron area-squared at most $10^{-24}$ in the model's coordinate units. Element shape therefore requires its own criterion in a downstream analysis.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The practical value of the framework is the connection between semantic recognition and editable geometry. Face-level predictions identify where an edit should act, while the retained boundary and adjacency relations support local removal and surface reconstruction. Processing the B-Rep before meshing reduces the geometric partitions that the mesher must resolve and keeps the simplified model available for subsequent CAD operations.

The paired measurements show a mean reduction of 20.88\% in surface triangles and 36.29\% in median mesh-generation time across nine aircraft under the stated controls. These results describe the meshing stage after CAD preparation and support targeted simplification in simulation model preparation.

For numerical simulation, the choice of details to remove must follow the physical problem and the required accuracy \parencite{antolin2024analysis_aware_defeaturing}. Further assessment should measure geometric deviation, element quality, complete preparation time, and simulation response under specified boundary conditions.

\section{Conclusions}

We presented a graph learning framework that connects face recognition in aircraft B-Reps to targeted removal and surface reconstruction. The network combines geometric attributes, shared-boundary relations, and context from the aircraft to distinguish background, rivets, and cosmetic features. Graph re-extraction maintains face correspondence during editing.

\begin{samepage}
Under fixed meshing settings, processing nine aircraft models reduced the surface-triangle count by a mean of 20.88\% and median mesh-generation time by a mean of 36.29\%. These findings show that simplifying selected CAD details can reduce the surface-meshing workload in simulation preparation while retaining an editable geometric model. Future work should relate geometric deviation and mesh quality to simulation accuracy and measure the complete preparation workflow.
\end{samepage}
