\begin{abstract}
  B-Rep geometric feature recognition underpins CAD editing and analysis and has been widely studied for shape-defined features such as fillets, chamfers, and holes. In complete-aircraft industrial simulation, recognising rivet and small-feature faces remains important because their small radii, boundaries, and local curvature trigger local mesh refinement under fixed deflection tolerances, increasing triangle counts and generation time. Their removal must remain associated with editable B-Rep faces to support local surface reconstruction and more continuous exterior surfaces for mesh preparation. This paper presents a face-level graph-learning framework for rivet and small-feature recognition. Each B-Rep face is represented as a graph node, and shared boundaries define neighbourhood relations. Two class-specific graph neural networks learn separate decision functions for the rivet and small-feature classes, while exported predictions remain associated with their source B-Rep faces for staged processing and inspection. The framework was evaluated on the full test set of 34,162 faces; rivet and small-feature F1 scores were 0.9312 and 0.8091. Under fixed meshing settings, original--simplified pairs showed reductions of 0.57\%--48.66\% in surface-triangle count and 2.49\%--58.29\% in median mesh-generation time. These results document face-level recognition, CAD-processing candidates, and paired mesh changes under the tested conditions; operation-level completeness, transferability to other CAD sources, and preservation of simulation response require further validation.

\section{Introduction}

Complete-aircraft B-Rep models retain the geometric and topological entities needed for design and analysis, but they also contain numerous local structures that may need to be inspected or processed separately. In the evaluated aircraft, rivet and small-feature faces account for a small fraction of the faces, while background surfaces dominate the face count. Recognising these rare targets also requires each prediction to remain linked to the CAD entity on which any subsequent geometric operation would act.

The rivet is enlarged because it is not visually discernible in the complete-aircraft views.}

Traditional CAD feature-recognition methods use geometric rules, attributed adjacency graphs, decomposition, or hint-based reasoning to identify manufacturing features \parencite{han2000manufacturing,shah2001discourse}. More recently, learning methods have operated directly on B-Rep geometry and topology, enabling face-level prediction without relying exclusively on intermediate meshes or point clouds \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet,colligan2022hierarchical,wu2024aagnet}. These studies establish the value of B-Rep-aware representations, but their typical objects and labels are mechanical parts, modelling-operation semantics, or machining features rather than rivets and heterogeneous small-features on complete aircraft.

Rare target faces make class-level errors easy to overlook in aggregate accuracy \parencite{he2009learning}. Free-form surfaces and differences in B-Rep face partitioning also limit what an isolated face reveals. When predictions guide removal or reconstruction, a false positive on a large smooth background face can carry greater geometric risk than a missed local target. These conditions call for per-aircraft error counts and the area of misclassified background faces. The complete aircraft is used as the partitioning unit so that related faces and local subgraphs remain together \parencite{kapoor2023leakage}.

We develop a face-level graph-learning and geometry-processing framework for recognising rivet and small-feature faces in complete-aircraft B-Rep models. It combines geometric and topological face attributes in two class-specific GNNs, fuses the branch scores into a three-class prediction, applies conservative structural constraints to high-risk candidates, and supplies the retained candidates to staged feature removal and local reconstruction.

This study contributes:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item A face-level B-Rep graph representation is constructed for three-class prediction: \textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, and \textit{small-feature}. The \textit{small-feature} class groups the annotated non-rivet target faces.
  \item Two class-specific GNNs use separate parameters for rivet and small-feature prediction and fuse their scores into a three-class label with rivet precedence.
  \item Training and testing are partitioned by complete aircraft rather than by randomly selected faces. In addition to pooled classification measures, the evaluation reports target-class support, missed target faces, and F1 for each aircraft, alongside background false-positive counts and their maximum relative face area.
  \item Conservative candidate checks restrict which predicted faces enter geometry processing, while the evaluation reports remaining background false positives for each aircraft.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec2}

For small-feature processing in complete aircraft B-Rep models, relevant studies mainly arise from two directions: three-dimensional scene segmentation and CAD feature recognition. The former focuses on locating semantic or instance regions in sampled 3D data, whereas the latter concerns recognising, within editable models, entities that can support subsequent geometric operations. For industrial CAD models, obtaining only region-level recognition results is insufficient to directly support feature removal or reconstruction; the results must also correspond to parametric faces and their topological relations. The following review therefore considers three-dimensional scene segmentation, CAD feature recognition, and the requirements of small-geometry recognition for downstream simulation processing.

\subsection{Three-Dimensional Deep Learning and Scene Segmentation}

Three-dimensional deep learning has developed several representations for semantic and instance segmentation, including voxels, point clouds, polygon meshes, and multi-view images. PointNet established a direct route to point-level segmentation by learning from unordered point sets \parencite{qi2017pointnet}. Later open-vocabulary methods incorporated image evidence into reconstructed 3D scenes. OpenMask3D fuses features from multiple views for each 3D instance proposal, whereas Open3DIS lifts two-dimensional instance masks to point-cloud regions and merges proposals across views \parencite{takmaz2023openmask3d,nguyen2024open3dis}. Together, these approaches illustrate how image features and multi-view projection can extend recognition beyond a fixed set of training labels.

The output of OpenMask3D and Open3DIS is a semantic or instance region derived from reconstructed point clouds and posed RGB-D images, typically in indoor or otherwise reconstructed scenes \parencite{takmaz2023openmask3d,nguyen2024open3dis}. Such a region is not, by itself, a reference to the parametric faces or topological relations of an editable CAD solid. Consequently, using a scene-segmentation result for local geometry removal would require an additional mapping from the detected region back to the B-Rep. CAD feature-recognition methods are designed around this entity-level requirement and provide a more direct starting point for editable-model operations.

\subsection{CAD Feature Recognition from Rules to B-Rep Learning}

CAD feature-recognition research has traditionally focused on extracting manufacturing information and supporting computer-aided process planning. Within this line of work, geometric rules, attributed adjacency graphs, volumetric decomposition and recomposition, and feature hints have been used to identify local structures such as holes, slots, and bosses \parencite{han2000manufacturing,shah2001discourse,babic2008review}. Their main advantage is interpretability when feature definitions and topological templates are explicit. Their transfer becomes more difficult, however, when features interact, thresholds are domain specific, surfaces are free-form, or the same design intent is represented by different face partitions \parencite{shah2001discourse}. Attributed adjacency graphs offer a natural bridge to learning-based recognition because they preserve faces together with their topological relationships. However, conventional subgraph matching remains tied to predefined patterns, which limits its adaptability when topology varies or features interact.

Recent CAD-learning methods retain B-Rep entities and their relations as part of the learning representation, thereby moving beyond recognition based solely on hand-crafted templates. UV-Net combines parametric surface and curve information with adjacency-graph topology, while BRepNet performs message passing over faces, edges, and oriented coedges for modelling-operation segmentation \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet}. Hierarchical CADNet augments a B-Rep face-adjacency graph with a surface-mesh level and edge convexity for machining-feature recognition, and AAGNet investigates multi-task machining-feature recognition on attributed adjacency graphs \parencite{colligan2022hierarchical,wu2024aagnet}. These studies establish several effective ways to expose B-Rep geometry and topology to face-level learning. The problem setting remains different from the one considered here: their datasets and labels focus mainly on mechanical parts, synthetic CAD collections, modelling-operation semantics, or machining features, rather than distinguishing \textit{background} faces, \textit{rivet} faces, and diverse \textit{small-feature} faces in complete-aircraft models.

\subsection{Gap for Small Geometry in Industrial Simulation Workflows}

In industrial simulation workflows, recognising a local feature is only an intermediate step: the result may determine whether that feature is retained, removed, or reconstructed before meshing and analysis. CAD simplification and analysis-aware defeaturing have examined the geometric consequences of feature removal \parencite{thakur2009cad_simplification,antolin2024analysis_aware_defeaturing}, and B-Rep learning has shown that face-based predictions can remain linked to their source entities \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet,yeo2021machining}. These two strands address complementary requirements, but the literature provides limited documentation of a route that identifies small, heterogeneous local geometry on complete industrial models and carries face-level predictions into CAD processing.

Aggregate segmentation accuracy does not indicate which background faces may be modified incorrectly. A false positive on a large smooth shell face, for example, may be more consequential to subsequent geometry processing than a missed local target. This motivates a B-Rep face-level formulation in which predictions can be located on the source model and errors are reported for each aircraft. In the representation developed next, faces serve as graph nodes and shared boundaries provide local context.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:preliminaries}

\subsection{B-Rep topology and geometry}\label{sec:brep_preliminary}

A boundary representation (B-Rep) describes a solid by linking topological entities---vertices, edges, wires (or loops), faces, shells, and solids---to their underlying geometric entities \parencite{iso10303_42_2025,li2026robust_tessellation}. A face commonly uses a parametric surface as its support, while an outer loop and any inner loops trim that support to a finite visible region. The trimming boundaries may be analytic or B-spline curves, and the supporting surface may likewise be a B-spline surface. Neighbouring faces can consequently lie on similar surfaces while differing in trimming loops and adjacency relations. B-Rep topology retains these relations and keeps each face associated with a specific entity in the editable solid, whereas an unstructured point set does not directly preserve either property. Figure~\ref{fig:brep_preliminary} illustrates the entity hierarchy used in the subsequent graph formulation.

Geometry and topology have different roles in this representation. Geometry supplies the point, curve, and surface descriptions from which positions, lengths, curvatures, and normals can be evaluated. Topology records how those geometric entities are bounded, oriented, and assembled: vertices bound edges, ordered edges form loops, loops bound faces, and connected faces form shells. A shared topological edge therefore indicates adjacency even when the two incident faces use different supporting surfaces or parameterisations. Conversely, similar surface geometry does not by itself imply that two faces are adjacent. This distinction is central to a face-level graph because node information is associated with individual faces, whereas graph connectivity follows topological incidence.

\subsection{Face-adjacency graphs}\label{sec:face_graph_preliminary}

The topology of a B-Rep induces a face-adjacency graph $G=(V,E)$. Each node $v_i\in V$ corresponds to one B-Rep face $f_i$, and an edge $(v_i,v_j)\in E$ indicates that faces $f_i$ and $f_j$ share a boundary. This construction converts the incidence relations of the solid into a graph without replacing the underlying CAD entities. Consecutive node indices may be introduced for tensor operations while the one-to-one correspondence with the source faces is maintained.

An attributed face graph associates a node vector $\mathbf{x}_i$ with each face and an edge vector $\mathbf{e}_{ij}$ with each adjacency. Node attributes can describe the geometry, scale, boundary organisation, or surface type of an individual face. Edge attributes describe relations between neighbouring faces, such as the length or type of their shared boundary, their relative scale, or their local angular relation. The graph representation thus separates properties of one face from properties of its connection to another face. Face-level graph constructions of this kind have been used to expose B-Rep geometry and topology to learning models \parencite{yeo2021machining,zhang2024brep2seq}.

The graph is independent of the order in which faces are enumerated. Renumbering the nodes changes their tensor positions but not the adjacency relation or the geometric meaning of the corresponding faces. The one-to-one node--face correspondence also distinguishes a face-adjacency graph from a sampled point graph, in which several samples may represent one face and the original trimming topology is not explicit.

\subsection{Message passing on attributed graphs}\label{sec:message_passing_preliminary}

A message-passing layer updates a node representation by combining its current state with information from adjacent nodes and edges. In a general form,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_i^{(\ell)}=
\operatorname{AGG}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}
\Phi^{(\ell)}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell)},\mathbf{h}_j^{(\ell)},\mathbf{e}_{ij}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell+1)}=
\Psi^{(\ell)}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell)},\mathbf{m}_i^{(\ell)}\right),
\label{eq:generic_message_passing}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}(i)$ is the neighbourhood of node $i$, $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ produces a message for one adjacency, $\operatorname{AGG}$ is a permutation-invariant aggregation, and $\Psi^{(\ell)}$ updates the node state. Repeated layers expand the receptive field beyond immediately adjacent faces while retaining a representation for every original face node.

When the relation between two faces is informative, the message transformation can depend on $\mathbf{e}_{ij}$ rather than treating every adjacency identically. Edge-conditioned convolution implements this idea by generating or modulating the message filter from the edge attributes \parencite{simonovsky2017dynamic}. Residual connections can preserve the preceding node state across multiple layers, and a pooled graph representation can supply model-level context alongside local face information. These operations are general graph-learning components; the task definition, selected attributes, network branches, decision rule, and training procedure used in this study are specified in Section~\ref{sec:methods}.

Message passing and graph pooling serve different purposes. Message passing preserves a state for each node and expands the local context available to that state as layers are stacked. A permutation-invariant pooling operation instead summarises a set of node states into one graph-level vector. Node-level classification requires the updated representation of each face to remain available, although graph-level context can be combined with it when the prediction also depends on properties of the complete model. The method developed next uses these concepts for face-level outputs while keeping the implementation-specific architecture outside this preliminary section.

\section{Face-Level Graph Learning}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Supervised face-level task}\label{sec:data_preparation}

The supervised task uses three mutually exclusive face labels: \textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, and \textit{small-feature}. In this class convention, \textit{small-feature} denotes annotated non-rivet target faces considered for subsequent simplification or reconstruction. Rivets retain their own target label. Annotation records associate each label with its original B-Rep face, as described in Section~\ref{sec:data_partition}.

For a complete-aircraft B-Rep, the learning objective is to assign one of three classes---\textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, or \textit{small-feature}---to every original face $v_i$. The predicted class and score can then be aligned with truth labels, inspected on the source model, and passed to later geometry processing. Intermediate removal models are treated separately in the staged processing workflow of Section~\ref{sec:geometry_protocol} because geometric operations change their topology and face enumeration.

\subsection{Face graph and input attributes}\label{sec:face_graph}

Using the construction introduced in Section~\ref{sec:face_graph_preliminary}, we instantiate one graph for each complete-aircraft B-Rep model. Each node represents one original face, while each graph edge represents a shared boundary. The present node and edge attributes, labels, and evaluation setting address the aircraft task defined in Section~\ref{sec:data_preparation}.

Figure~\ref{fig:face_graph_attributes} illustrates node and edge attributes and neighbour aggregation on the face graph. Its colours distinguish face nodes, not prediction classes. The resulting graph provides each face node with 48 attributes and each graph edge with six attributes to the class-specific GNNs introduced in Section~\ref{sec:dual_gnn}.

The current implementation assigns 48 node attributes to each B-Rep face. The attributes are grouped by information source rather than used as hand-coded recognition rules: face scale and shape include relative area, perimeter, compactness, and relations to neighbouring-face areas; surface information includes curvature, radius availability, and surface type; boundary organisation includes wire, inner-wire, and edge counts; and local-neighbourhood statistics describe adjacent surface types, convex, concave, or smooth relations, normal similarity, and same-surface connectivity. Model-scale-normalised geometric quantities and relative positional descriptors are also included. The resulting vector characterises the local state of a B-Rep face, but no single area, surface type, or curvature value prescribes a \textit{rivet} or \textit{small-feature} label.

Rivets often have repeated local patterns, whereas small-feature instances vary in scale, surface form, and attachment mode. Neither face area nor surface type alone is therefore a reliable class rule. Aircraft models also differ in physical scale and face partitioning; without normalisation, geometric magnitudes may reflect model size or export conventions. We normalise quantities that require scale consistency and reuse the feature order and statistics computed from the training set. The evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:results} tests this representation on the fixed aircraft partition, not on arbitrary CAD sources.

Each face adjacency is associated with six edge attributes: connection type, area ratio, neighbouring surface type, shared-edge length, mean dihedral angle, and dihedral-angle standard deviation. These attributes describe the boundary relation between two B-Rep faces rather than either face in isolation. For example, shared-edge length characterises the local scale of an adjacency, dihedral-angle statistics describe local turning or continuity, and neighbouring surface type and area ratio provide relational information unavailable from a single node. Following the edge-conditioned formulation in Section~\ref{sec:message_passing_preliminary}, these attributes condition the learned message transformations defined in Section~\ref{sec:class_specific_learning}.

Message passing combines each face's attributes with those of adjacent faces and their shared boundaries. This supplies local geometric and topological context when a single face's area, curvature, or surface type is ambiguous. Both class-specific branches use the same graph input but learn separate representations, as described in Section~\ref{sec:dual_gnn}.

The three class labels define the prediction target, while node and edge attributes supply the learning input. The one-to-one node--face correspondence allows the resulting labels to be compared with annotations, visualised on the B-Rep model, and passed to geometry processing.

\subsection{Class-Specific Graph Learning}\label{sec:class_specific_learning}

\subsubsection{Class-specific network architecture}\label{sec:dual_gnn}

The recognition model comprises two class-specific, edge-conditioned GNNs: a rivet branch and a small-feature branch. Both receive the same B-Rep face graph and attributes, but each has its own node encoder, edge encoder, graph-convolution layers, adapter, and scalar output head. Thus, they share the input representation and graph connectivity, not a learned graph encoder. The network produces two binary scores; the fixed decision rule in Section~\ref{sec:training_inference} converts them into one of the three face labels.

Rivets and small-features differ in local geometry and attachment patterns. We therefore formulate recognition as two class-specific one-vs-rest detection tasks. Independent branches allow each target category to learn its own neighbourhood representation, with separately assigned loss weights and decision thresholds. To assess this design against direct three-class prediction, we trained a unified three-class softmax GNN as a comparison model. Section~\ref{sec:architecture_comparison} reports higher F1 scores for both target classes and fewer background false positives for the dual-branch system, providing empirical support for retaining this design under the reported training and evaluation protocol. Figure~\ref{fig:class_specific_gnn} separates the learned network components from the fixed threshold decision.

For class-specific branch $k\in\{r,s\}$, where $r$ denotes rivet and $s$ denotes small-feature, the raw node and edge attributes are first transformed by separate encoders:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(0)}=\phi_{x,k}(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{ij,k}=\phi_{e,k}(\mathbf{e}_{ij}).
\label{eq:class_specific_input_encoding}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{48}$ describes face node $v_i$, and $\mathbf{e}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}$ describes its adjacency to face $j$. Each encoder comprises a linear transformation, LayerNorm, and ReLU, preserving the respective input width. The parameters of $\phi_{x,r}$, $\phi_{e,r}$, $\phi_{x,s}$, and $\phi_{e,s}$ are distinct.

Each branch applies four NNConv layers with hidden width 64. Layer-specific edge MLPs transform the encoded adjacency attributes into weights for neighbour messages, following the edge-conditioned formulation \parencite{simonovsky2017dynamic}. Each convolution is followed by LayerNorm, ReLU, and dropout:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_{i,k}^{(\ell+1)}=
\operatorname{Dropout}\!\left(
\operatorname{ReLU}\!\left(
\operatorname{LN}\!\left(
\operatorname{NNConv}_{k}
(\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(\ell)},\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{ij,k})
\right)\right)\right).
\label{eq:class_specific_message_update}
\end{equation}
The first layer maps 48 features to 64 and has no residual addition. Layers 2--4 retain width 64 and add their input states:
\[
\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(\ell+1)}=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{u}_{i,k}^{(1)}, & \ell=0,\\
\mathbf{u}_{i,k}^{(\ell+1)}+\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(\ell)}, & \ell=1,2,3.
\end{cases}
\]

\begin{samepage}
After the fourth layer, each face has a 64-dimensional local representation. Element-wise mean and max pooling over the current aircraft's face nodes $V$ each yield 64 features. Their concatenation supplies 128-dimensional global context, which is appended to every local representation:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{c}_k&=\left[\operatorname{Mean}_{j\in V}\mathbf{h}_{j,k}^{(4)}
\,\Vert\,\operatorname{Max}_{j\in V}\mathbf{h}_{j,k}^{(4)}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{128},\\
\mathbf{z}_{i,k}&=\left[\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(4)}\,\Vert\,\mathbf{c}_k\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{192},
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{samepage}
where $\Vert$ denotes concatenation. A branch-specific adapter $A_k$ maps 192 features to 96 using a linear layer, LayerNorm, ReLU, and dropout. A scalar linear head $g_k$ then maps 96 features to one logit. The two sigmoid scores are
\begin{equation}
p_{r,i}=\sigma\!\left(g_r(A_r(\mathbf{z}_{i,r}))\right), \quad
p_{s,i}=\sigma\!\left(g_s(A_s(\mathbf{z}_{i,s}))\right).
\label{eq:class_specific_scores}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Binary training objectives and fixed decision rule}\label{sec:training_inference}

The rivet branch is trained against all non-rivet faces, including small-features and background; the small-feature branch is trained against all non-small-feature faces, including rivets and background. The binary target pairs $(t_{i,r},t_{i,s})$ are $(1,0)$ for rivets, $(0,1)$ for small-features, and $(0,0)$ for background. This decomposes the mutually exclusive labels into two one-vs-rest learning tasks, without a separate background prediction head.

Target/non-target imbalance motivates loss weighting \parencite{buda2018imbalance}. Each branch uses a weighted binary focal loss with $\gamma=2.0$ to reduce the contribution of well-classified negative faces \parencite{lin2017focal}. Both branches are jointly optimised on the same complete-aircraft graphs, with objective $\mathcal{L}=(\mathcal{L}_r+\mathcal{L}_s)/2$, where each $\mathcal{L}_k$ is the mean weighted focal loss for that branch over the supervised faces. Their learned parameters remain separate; the threshold decision is not part of the training loss.

\begin{samepage}
At inference, each branch independently tests whether a face belongs to its target category. The two sigmoid scores are not normalised across classes and can both exceed their thresholds. Because the annotations assign mutually exclusive labels, a fixed rule converts these independent detections into one face label. This label-resolution step has no trainable fusion parameters:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i =
\begin{cases}
\textit{rivet}, & p_{r,i} \geq \tau_r, \\
\textit{small-feature}, & p_{r,i} < \tau_r \ \text{and} \ p_{s,i} \geq \tau_s, \\
\textit{background}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:dual_head_decision}
\end{equation}
\end{samepage}
The stored inference contract fixes $\tau_r=0.9$ and $\tau_s=0.5$. Under this rule, a face is background when neither threshold is met and receives the corresponding target label when only one is met. When both are met, the preset rivet-precedence policy resolves the conflict; it is not a learned comparison of score magnitudes. This policy follows the rivet-first processing order in Section~\ref{sec:geometry_protocol}, rather than asserting that it is optimal for every conflict. Applications requiring only two independent candidate sets could retain the branch detections without label resolution. Here, a single label is used to match the three-class annotations and face-level evaluation interface. Inference reuses the paired checkpoint's feature order, normalisation statistics, and thresholds. The branch scores and final label remain linked to the source face identifier for evaluation and staged CAD processing.

\section{Feature-Processing and Training Protocol}\label{sec:processing_pipeline}

\subsection{Conservative candidate filtering}\label{sec:risk_filtering}

Conservative post-processing examines only faces predicted as targets and returns a rejected candidate to \textit{background}; it never promotes a background prediction. The rivet guard rejects a predicted rivet when its relative face area exceeds $5\times10^{-6}$. The small-feature guard rejects specified wing or shell topologies computed from face geometry and local adjacency; for the original-model small-feature stage, an exterior-visibility check also rejects a candidate when none of its sampled face points has an unblocked ray along any of the six Cartesian directions. The reported face-level recognition labels additionally undergo a planar-patch rivet check and a small-feature screening pass, while the small-feature-only processing route uses a separate topology check. These checks define the reported labels and processing candidates, but do not measure operation success.

\subsection{Staged removal, reconstruction, and validation}\label{sec:geometry_protocol}

Each retained candidate carries the face identifier of the B-Rep state on which it was predicted; the processing stage uses this reference to locate the corresponding face for removal or local reconstruction. Prediction accuracy, operation success, and suitability for a particular engineering analysis nevertheless require separate checks; acceptance for simulation depends on the task, loads, boundary conditions, and regions that must remain intact \parencite{thakur2009cad_simplification,antolin2024analysis_aware_defeaturing}.

Removal or reconstruction changes topology and face enumeration. The workflow therefore first removes rivet candidates, re-extracts faces from the intermediate model, and then predicts small-features on that model. The second-stage predictions refer to this intermediate model rather than to the original model.

Figure~\ref{fig:geometry_protocol} illustrates the staged workflow on the Gulfstream G280 model. The horizontal sequence provides whole-aircraft context, while the enlarged local views show the selected feature regions at matched camera settings. The selected small-feature regions remain visible after the rivet stage and are addressed in the subsequent small-feature stage. For a second-stage small-feature candidate, the local host relation determines whether to remove, reconstruct, or repair the affected region.

For each original--simplified pair, we use the same OpenCASCADE surface mesher at both endpoints, with relative linear deflection 0.0005 and angular deflection 0.5 rad \parencite{occt_mesh_2026}. We record node and triangle counts and use the median generation time from five meshing runs per model. Measurements were obtained on Windows 10 Pro 25H2 with an Intel Core i7-14700KF, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 (8~GB), Python 3.10.20, OpenCASCADE 7.9.0, and PyTorch 2.12.1+cu130. These mesh-scale and timing measures do not test finite-element accuracy or physical-response equivalence.

For the post-meshing baseline, the original B-Rep is first meshed with the same OpenCASCADE settings and then simplified by quadric error metrics (QEM) edge collapse \parencite{garland1997qem}. For each model, the QEM target is the final triangle count produced by the B-Rep processing route. We use fast-simplification version 0.2.0 with aggregation factor 7.0 and border preservation disabled \parencite{fast_simplification_2026}. The reported baseline component is the recorded median time for meshing the original B-Rep plus the median of five QEM-kernel runs. It excludes mesh extraction and array conversion, file I/O, recognition, candidate filtering, and CAD operations. Thus, the comparison is a matched-triangle timing-component comparison, not an end-to-end workflow comparison.

The paired models support comparison of CAD-face and surface-mesh scale under fixed meshing controls. Removal completeness, reconstruction success, geometric fidelity, and physical-response equivalence require additional evidence.

\subsection{Data Annotation and Training Protocol}\label{sec:data_training}

\subsubsection{Annotation contract and model-level partitioning}\label{sec:data_partition}

Annotation records associate each original face with its source category. Rivet faces receive the \textit{rivet} label; the remaining annotated target faces receive the \textit{small-feature} label; and all other faces are labelled \textit{background}. This association allows each label to be checked against the corresponding B-Rep model before training or evaluation.

All faces from an aircraft remain in one partition, avoiding overlap of same-aircraft faces and local adjacency relations \parencite{kapoor2023leakage}. Model parameters and normalisation statistics are computed from the training partition. The full test set is processed with the stored feature order, statistics, class mapping, and decision thresholds; it is not used to update the reported checkpoint. Table~\ref{tab:data_composition} gives the training and test composition used for the reported face-level metrics.

\subsubsection{Training configuration and reproducibility}\label{sec:training_configuration}

Each aircraft is represented as one face graph. Training-set normalisation statistics, feature order, class mapping, network configuration, and decision thresholds are stored with the checkpoint and reused for inference. This keeps the preprocessing and prediction rules consistent across the reported training and test partitions.

The reported training run uses AdamW with an initial learning rate of 0.005, dropout of 0.2, weight decay of $10^{-4}$, focal-loss $\gamma=2.0$, seed 123, and 50 epochs over full-aircraft graphs at batch size 8. AdamW decouples weight decay from adaptive gradient updates \parencite{loshchilov2019adamw}. The model weights and training-set statistics are retained together as a paired checkpoint bundle.

The reported checkpoint uses 48 node fields organised into the five information groups in Table~\ref{tab:node_attribute_groups}; the eight surface-type indicators are one-hot fields for the surface types observed in the training contract. The graph also stores six edge attributes: edge type, area ratio, neighbour surface type, shared-boundary length, and the mean and standard deviation of dihedral angle.

\section{Experimental Results}\label{sec:results}

This section reports face-level recognition results, CAD-processing outputs, and surface-mesh comparisons before and after simplification. Recognition results use aligned prediction and truth records. The CAD table reports processing inputs at their respective stages; candidate outputs and visual examples do not measure operation-level completeness.

\subsection{Face-level recognition and background-risk characterisation}\label{sec:e1_results}

The fixed checkpoint was evaluated on all 34,162 faces in the full test set. Prediction and truth files were aligned face by face for each model, so the class metrics and aircraft-level error counts refer to the same faces. Epoch-wise histories are unavailable, and the reported values therefore characterise the stored checkpoint rather than training convergence.

For each class, precision ($P$) is the proportion of predicted faces in that class that are correct, whereas recall ($R$) is the proportion of true faces in that class that are recovered. The F1 score is their harmonic mean, $F1 = 2PR/(P+R)$, and therefore summarises the balance between false candidates and missed target faces.

The full-test results give F1 scores of 0.9312 for rivets and 0.8091 for small-features (Table~\ref{tab:e1_class_metrics}). Overall accuracy was 0.9900 and background F1 was 0.9949. Rivet precision and recall were 0.9436 and 0.9191, respectively. Small-feature precision and recall were 0.7841 and 0.8358, respectively. These class-level measures complement the background-dominated overall accuracy and show the remaining trade-off between missed target faces and false candidates.

\subsection{Discussion and evidence boundaries}\label{sec:experimental_boundaries}

The face-level evaluation shows that the proposed method can recognise rivet and small-feature faces in the reported complete-aircraft B-Rep test set, with F1 scores of 0.9312 and 0.8091, respectively. The staged-processing cases supply candidate inputs, and the nine original--simplified pairs show lower surface-triangle counts and median mesh-generation times under the tested settings. These records do not quantify how many individual CAD operations completed successfully.

The predictions should nevertheless be reviewed before geometric operations are accepted. Table~\ref{tab:e1_per_aircraft_targets} shows that target-class support and missed faces differ substantially across the nine aircraft, particularly for small-features; pooled F1 does not imply uniformly stable performance by aircraft. Residual background false positives also vary across models, and the small-feature branch has higher recall than precision, so some background faces remain among the candidate small-features. Candidate counts are operation inputs rather than feature-instance counts or completed removals. Because rivet processing changes face enumeration, the model is re-extracted before the second-stage prediction, which refers to the intermediate model rather than the original model.

Under fixed OpenCASCADE controls, the pairs reported in Table~\ref{tab:e3_mesh} have lower surface-triangle counts and median meshing times after simplification. The changes in triangle count and time are not proportional, indicating that triangle count alone does not determine meshing time. At matched triangle targets, Table~\ref{tab:qem_matched_time} also shows lower final-meshing components than original meshing plus the QEM kernel, under the explicitly limited timing accounting. These results describe CAD-face and mesh-scale changes under the stated settings; they do not establish end-to-end timing superiority, geometric fidelity, B-Rep equivalence, or preservation of analysis response.

\section{Conclusions}
This study developed a framework for face-level identification of rivet and small-feature faces in B-Rep models of complete aircraft. Each face and its shared-boundary relations are encoded as a graph, and two class-specific GNNs produce the three-class output. The predicted faces can be located on the corresponding original or intermediate B-Rep model for subsequent processing.

On the full test set of 34,162 faces, the framework achieved F1 scores of 0.9312 for rivets and 0.8091 for small-features. Across the reported original--simplified pairs, surface-triangle counts were 0.57\%--48.66\% lower and median mesh-generation times were 2.49\%--58.29\% lower under the same meshing settings. At matched triangle targets, final meshing after B-Rep processing was 7.22\%--60.05\% lower than the separately timed original-meshing-plus-QEM-kernel component. The reported predictions provide locatable CAD-processing candidates, and the paired results demonstrate lower mesh complexity and shorter meshing times for the tested models. These outcomes support reviewable CAD and mesh preparation; they do not quantify the effect of individual predictions or operations. Solver runtime and engineering response were not evaluated.

The present evidence does not establish transferability to other CAD sources, complete feature-removal success, end-to-end timing superiority over QEM, geometric fidelity, or equivalence for a downstream CAE task. Candidate-face counts are not operation success rates. Future work should evaluate external CAD sources, record the outcome of each removal and reconstruction attempt, compare both routes using matched quality criteria and complete timing, isolate component effects through repeated controlled experiments, and assess task-specific CAE responses under defined loads and boundary conditions. These extensions are required to determine when the geometric changes are acceptable for a particular analysis purpose.
