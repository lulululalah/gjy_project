\begin{abstract}
  B-Rep geometric feature recognition underpins CAD editing and analysis and has been widely studied for shape-defined features such as fillets, chamfers, and holes. In complete-aircraft industrial simulation, recognising rivet and surface-feature faces remains important because their small radii, boundaries, and local curvature trigger local mesh refinement under fixed deflection tolerances, increasing triangle counts and generation time. Their removal must remain associated with editable B-Rep faces to support local surface reconstruction and more continuous exterior surfaces for mesh preparation. This paper presents a face-level graph-learning framework for rivet and surface-feature recognition. Each B-Rep face is represented as a graph node, and shared boundaries define neighbourhood relations. Two class-specific graph neural networks learn separate decision functions for the rivet and surface-feature classes, while each exported prediction retains its original B-Rep face identifier to support staged processing and result inspection. The framework was evaluated on the full test set of 34,162 faces; rivet and surface-feature F1 scores were 0.9312 and 0.8091. Under fixed meshing settings, original--simplified pairs showed reductions of 0.57\%--48.66\% in surface-triangle count and 2.49\%--58.29\% in median mesh-generation time. These results document a traceable route from face-level recognition to geometric processing and mesh preparation, while transferability to other CAD sources, the operation-level completeness of feature removal, and the preservation of simulation response require further validation.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Complete-aircraft B-Rep models retain the geometric and topological entities needed for design and analysis, but they also contain numerous local structures that may need to be inspected or processed separately. In the evaluated aircraft, rivets, decals, windows, and related surface-features account for a small fraction of the faces, while background surfaces dominate the face count. Recognising these rare targets also requires each prediction to remain linked to the CAD entity on which any subsequent geometric operation would act.

Traditional CAD feature-recognition methods use geometric rules, attributed adjacency graphs, decomposition, or hint-based reasoning to identify manufacturing features \parencite{han2000manufacturing,shah2001discourse}. More recently, learning methods have operated directly on B-Rep geometry and topology, enabling face-level prediction without relying exclusively on intermediate meshes or point clouds \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet,colligan2022hierarchical,wu2024aagnet}. These studies establish the value of B-Rep-aware representations, but their typical objects and labels are mechanical parts, modelling-operation semantics, or machining features rather than rivets and heterogeneous surface-features on complete aircraft.

Rare target faces make class-level errors easy to overlook in aggregate accuracy \parencite{he2009learning}. Free-form surfaces and differences in B-Rep face partitioning also limit what an isolated face reveals. When predictions guide removal or reconstruction, a false positive on a large smooth background face can carry greater geometric risk than a missed local target. These conditions call for per-aircraft error counts, the area of misclassified background faces, and a record of the original face identifier. The complete aircraft is used as the partitioning unit so that related faces and local subgraphs remain together \parencite{kapoor2023leakage}.

We develop a face-level graph-learning and geometry-processing framework for recognising rivets and other surface-features in complete-aircraft B-Rep models. It combines geometric and topological face attributes in two class-specific GNNs, fuses the branch scores into a three-class prediction, applies conservative structural constraints to high-risk candidates, and preserves the correspondence between each prediction and its face identifier for subsequent feature removal and local reconstruction.

This study contributes:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item A face-level B-Rep graph representation is constructed for three-class prediction: \textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, and \textit{surface-feature}. The \textit{surface-feature} class encompasses non-rivet small surface-features, including decals and windows.
  \item Two class-specific GNNs use separate parameters for rivet and surface-feature prediction and fuse their scores into a three-class label with rivet precedence.
  \item Training and testing are partitioned by complete aircraft rather than by randomly selected faces. In addition to conventional classification measures, the evaluation considers, for each aircraft, the number of background faces falsely identified as features and the maximum area of a falsely identified background face.
  \item The original face identifier is retained for every prediction, allowing the predicted label to be traced back to a specific geometric entity and used in subsequent feature removal, host-face localisation, and host-face reconstruction.
  \item Conservative candidate checks address the risk of sending large smooth shell faces to downstream geometry operations, with remaining background false positives reported for each aircraft.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec2}

For small-feature processing in complete aircraft B-Rep models, relevant studies mainly arise from two directions: three-dimensional scene segmentation and CAD feature recognition. The former focuses on locating semantic or instance regions in sampled 3D data, whereas the latter concerns recognising, within editable models, entities that can support subsequent geometric operations. For industrial CAD models, obtaining only region-level recognition results is insufficient to directly support feature removal or reconstruction; the results must also correspond to parametric faces and their topological relations. The following review therefore considers three-dimensional scene segmentation, CAD feature recognition, and the requirements of small-geometry recognition for downstream simulation processing.

\subsection{Three-Dimensional Deep Learning and Scene Segmentation}

Three-dimensional deep learning has developed several representations for semantic and instance segmentation, including voxels, point clouds, polygon meshes, and multi-view images. PointNet established a direct route to point-level segmentation by learning from unordered point sets \parencite{qi2017pointnet}. Later open-vocabulary methods incorporated image evidence into reconstructed 3D scenes. OpenMask3D fuses features from multiple views for each 3D instance proposal, whereas Open3DIS lifts two-dimensional instance masks to point-cloud regions and merges proposals across views \parencite{takmaz2023openmask3d,nguyen2024open3dis}. Together, these approaches illustrate how image features and multi-view projection can extend recognition beyond a fixed set of training labels.

The output of OpenMask3D and Open3DIS is a semantic or instance region derived from reconstructed point clouds and posed RGB-D images, typically in indoor or otherwise reconstructed scenes \parencite{takmaz2023openmask3d,nguyen2024open3dis}. Such a region is not, by itself, a reference to the parametric faces or topological relations of an editable CAD solid. Consequently, using a scene-segmentation result for local geometry removal would require an additional mapping from the detected region back to the B-Rep. CAD feature-recognition methods are designed around this entity-level requirement and provide a more direct starting point for editable-model operations.

\subsection{CAD Feature Recognition from Rules to B-Rep Learning}

CAD feature-recognition research has traditionally focused on extracting manufacturing information and supporting computer-aided process planning. Within this line of work, geometric rules, attributed adjacency graphs, volumetric decomposition and recomposition, and feature hints have been used to identify local structures such as holes, slots, and bosses \parencite{han2000manufacturing,shah2001discourse,babic2008review}. Their main advantage is interpretability when feature definitions and topological templates are explicit. Their transfer becomes more difficult, however, when features interact, thresholds are domain specific, surfaces are free-form, or the same design intent is represented by different face partitions \parencite{shah2001discourse}. Attributed adjacency graphs offer a natural bridge to learning-based recognition because they preserve faces together with their topological relationships. However, conventional subgraph matching remains tied to predefined patterns, which limits its adaptability when topology varies or features interact.

Recent CAD-learning methods retain B-Rep entities and their relations as part of the learning representation, thereby moving beyond recognition based solely on hand-crafted templates. UV-Net combines parametric surface and curve information with adjacency-graph topology, while BRepNet performs message passing over faces, edges, and oriented coedges for modelling-operation segmentation \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet}. Hierarchical CADNet augments a B-Rep face-adjacency graph with a surface-mesh level and edge convexity for machining-feature recognition, and AAGNet investigates multi-task machining-feature recognition on attributed adjacency graphs \parencite{colligan2022hierarchical,wu2024aagnet}. These studies establish several effective ways to expose B-Rep geometry and topology to face-level learning. The problem setting remains different from the one considered here: their datasets and labels focus mainly on mechanical parts, synthetic CAD collections, modelling-operation semantics, or machining features, rather than distinguishing \textit{background} faces, \textit{rivet} faces, and diverse \textit{surface-feature} faces in complete-aircraft models.

\subsection{Gap for Small Geometry in Industrial Simulation Workflows}

In industrial simulation workflows, recognising a local feature is only an intermediate step: the result may determine whether that feature is retained, removed, or reconstructed before meshing and analysis. CAD simplification and analysis-aware defeaturing have examined the geometric consequences of feature removal \parencite{thakur2009cad_simplification,antolin2024analysis_aware_defeaturing}, and B-Rep learning has shown that face-based predictions can remain linked to their source entities \parencite{jayaraman2021uvnet,lambourne2021brepnet,yeo2021machining}. These two strands address complementary requirements, but the literature provides limited documentation of an end-to-end route that identifies small, heterogeneous local geometry on complete industrial models and converts the predictions into traceable, risk-aware CAD operations.

Aggregate segmentation accuracy does not indicate which background faces may be modified incorrectly. A false positive on a large smooth shell face, for example, may be more consequential to subsequent geometry processing than a missed local target. This motivates a B-Rep face-level formulation in which the face identifier is carried into the processing stage and errors are reported for each aircraft. The representation developed next follows directly from this requirement: faces serve as graph nodes, shared boundaries provide local context, and the face identifier preserves the link between a prediction and its CAD entity.

\section{B-Rep Preliminaries and Graph Learning}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{B-Rep preliminary}\label{sec:brep_preliminary}

A boundary representation (B-Rep) describes a solid by linking topological entities---vertices, edges, wires (or loops), faces, shells, and solids---to their underlying geometric entities \parencite{iso10303_42_2025}. A face commonly uses a parametric surface as its support, while an outer loop and any inner loops trim that support to a finite visible region. The trimming boundaries may be analytic or B-spline curves, and the supporting surface may likewise be a B-spline surface. Neighbouring faces can consequently lie on similar surfaces while differing in trimming loops and adjacency relations. These relations are retained by the B-Rep but are not directly available once the model is reduced to an unstructured point set. They provide the information required to represent each prediction as both a learning target and a locatable CAD entity. Figure~\ref{fig:brep_preliminary} illustrates this interpretation.

\subsection{Supervised face-level task}\label{sec:data_preparation}

Small geometry is used here only as an operational label for supervised learning, not as a feature-recognition rule. The annotation protocol assigns each original B-Rep face to \textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, or \textit{surface-feature}; the latter includes non-rivet targets such as decals and windows that are considered in subsequent simplification or reconstruction. Membership is supplied by the annotation records rather than inferred from a fixed area, curvature, or topological template. The detailed annotation procedure and model-level partitioning are given in Section~\ref{sec:data_partition}.

For a complete-aircraft B-Rep, the learning objective is to assign one of three classes---\textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, or \textit{surface-feature}---to every original face $v_i$, while retaining its face identifier. The predicted class and score are exported with that face identifier, allowing the output to be aligned with truth labels, inspected on the source model, and passed to later geometry processing. Intermediate removal models establish new sets of face identifiers and are therefore treated separately in the staged processing workflow of Section~\ref{sec:geometry_protocol}.

\subsection{Face graph and input attributes}\label{sec:face_graph}

For each complete-aircraft B-Rep model, we construct a graph $G=(V,E)$ in which node $v_i\in V$ represents one original face and retains its face identifier; an edge in $E$ represents a shared boundary. Node attributes describe individual faces, while edge attributes describe their relations. The face-level representation follows earlier B-Rep learning work \parencite{yeo2021machining,zhang2024brep2seq}; the present labels and evaluation setting address the aircraft task defined in Section~\ref{sec:data_preparation}.

Figure~\ref{fig:face_graph_attributes} shows how neighbouring faces become graph nodes connected by shared boundaries. Its colours distinguish faces, not prediction classes. The resulting graph provides each face node with 48 attributes and each graph edge with six attributes to the class-specific GNNs introduced in Section~\ref{sec:dual_gnn}.

The current implementation assigns 48 node attributes to each B-Rep face. The attributes are grouped by information source rather than used as hand-coded recognition rules: face scale and shape include relative area, perimeter, compactness, and relations to neighbouring-face areas; surface information includes curvature, radius availability, and surface type; boundary organisation includes wire, inner-wire, and edge counts; and local-neighbourhood statistics describe adjacent surface types, convex, concave, or smooth relations, normal similarity, and same-surface connectivity. Model-scale-normalised geometric quantities and relative positional descriptors are also included. The resulting vector characterises the local state of a B-Rep face, but no single area, surface type, or curvature value prescribes a \textit{rivet} or \textit{surface-feature} label.

Rivets often have repeated local patterns, whereas surface-feature instances vary in scale, surface form, and attachment mode. Neither face area nor surface type alone is therefore a reliable class rule. Aircraft models also differ in physical scale and face partitioning; without normalisation, geometric magnitudes may reflect model size or export conventions. We normalise quantities that require scale consistency and reuse the feature order and statistics computed from the training set. The evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:results} tests this representation on the fixed aircraft partition, not on arbitrary CAD sources.

Each face adjacency is associated with six edge attributes: connection type, area ratio, neighbouring surface type, shared-edge length, mean dihedral angle, and dihedral-angle standard deviation. These attributes describe the boundary relation between two B-Rep faces rather than either face in isolation. For example, shared-edge length characterises the local scale of an adjacency, dihedral-angle statistics describe local turning or continuity, and neighbouring surface type and area ratio provide relational information unavailable from a single node. Edge-conditioned graph convolutions provide a general basis for retaining such information during neighbourhood aggregation, because their filters can be conditioned on edge labels \parencite{simonovsky2017dynamic}. In the present work, this observation motivates supplying edge attributes to message passing; the class-specific encoders and their learned edge-conditioned operations are defined in Section~\ref{sec:class_specific_learning}.

Message passing combines each face's attributes with those of adjacent faces and their shared boundaries. This supplies local geometric and topological context when a single face's area, curvature, or surface type is ambiguous. Both class-specific branches use the same graph input but learn separate representations, as described in Section~\ref{sec:dual_gnn}.

Graph nodes may receive consecutive indices for tensor operations, but the export restores each prediction to its original face identifier. This allows face-level labels to be compared with annotations, visualised on the original B-Rep model, and passed to geometry processing. The three class labels define the prediction target; node and edge attributes supply the learning input.

\subsection{Class-Specific Graph Learning}\label{sec:class_specific_learning}

\subsubsection{Network architecture and decision fusion}\label{sec:dual_gnn}

The classifier has a rivet branch and a surface-feature branch. They receive the same graph input but have separate node encoders, edge encoders, graph-convolution layers, adapters, and output heads. Each branch learns a binary target-versus-background decision, and their scores are combined into the three-class output. Separate parameters allow the branches to learn different patterns.

Figure~\ref{fig:class_specific_gnn} shows the two branches, score fusion, and the face identifier traceability path. The face identifier connects graph nodes to exported predictions and is not a neural-network input feature.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_class_specific_gnn_architecture_v3.png}
\caption{Class-specific GNNs for face-level three-class prediction with face identifier traceability. The same B-Rep face graph, node attributes, and edge attributes enter parameter-independent rivet and surface-feature branches. Each branch contains separate attribute encoders, four residual edge-conditioned graph-convolution layers, an adapter, and an output head. The two sigmoid scores are fused using rivet precedence to produce \textit{background}, \textit{rivet}, or \textit{surface-feature}. The dashed traceability path connects the original face entity and exported prediction, but is not a learned feature.}
\label{fig:class_specific_gnn}
\end{figure}

For class-specific branch $k\in\{r,s\}$, where $r$ denotes rivet and $s$ denotes surface-feature, the raw node and edge attributes are first transformed by separate encoders:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(0)}=\phi_{x,k}(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{ij,k}=\phi_{e,k}(\mathbf{e}_{ij}).
\label{eq:class_specific_input_encoding}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf{x}_i$ is the 48-dimensional attribute vector of B-Rep face node $v_i$, $\mathbf{e}_{ij}$ is the six-dimensional attribute vector of the adjacency between faces $i$ and $j$, and $\phi_{x,k}$ and $\phi_{e,k}$ are the class-specific node and edge encoders. Each encoder comprises a linear transformation, LayerNorm, and ReLU activation. The input attributes are identical for the two branches, but the parameters of $\phi_{x,r}$, $\phi_{e,r}$, $\phi_{x,s}$, and $\phi_{e,s}$ are distinct.

Each branch uses four residual edge-conditioned graph-convolution layers with hidden dimension 64. At layer $\ell$, the encoded edge attributes condition the message transformation used by \texttt{NNConv}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_{i,k}^{(\ell+1)}=
\operatorname{Dropout}\!\left(
\operatorname{ReLU}\!\left(
\operatorname{LN}\!\left(
\operatorname{NNConv}_{k}
(\mathbf{h}_{i,k}^{(\ell)},\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{ij,k})
\right)\right)\right).
\label{eq:class_specific_message_update}
\end{equation}
Residual connections preserve a node state while its receptive field expands. After four layers, the local representation is concatenated with pooled complete-aircraft context, mapped by a class-specific adapter, and passed to one output head. Edge-conditioned filtering conditions message transformations on edge labels \parencite{simonovsky2017dynamic}; the present attributes, branches, and decision rule remain application-specific.

Let $\mathbf{z}_{i,r}$ and $\mathbf{z}_{i,s}$ be the two class-specific representations for face $i$. Their independent sigmoid scores are
\begin{equation}
p_{r,i}=\sigma(g_r(\mathbf{z}_{i,r})), \quad
p_{s,i}=\sigma(g_s(\mathbf{z}_{i,s})),
\label{eq:class_specific_scores}
\end{equation}
where $g_r$ and $g_s$ are output heads. The two scores are converted to the final face label with rivet precedence:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i =
\begin{cases}
\textit{rivet}, & p_{r,i} \geq \tau_r, \\
\textit{surface-feature}, & p_{r,i} < \tau_r \ \text{and} \ p_{s,i} \geq \tau_s, \\
\textit{background}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:dual_head_decision}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}_i$ is the final class. The fixed inference contract uses $\tau_r=0.9$ and $\tau_s=0.5$; the rivet decision cannot be overwritten by the surface-feature score. The paired model statistics file preserves these thresholds and the feature-processing contract.

\subsubsection{Learning objective and inference decision}\label{sec:training_inference}

The two branches are jointly optimised on the same complete-aircraft graphs as two binary tasks: background--rivet and background--surface-feature. Each head uses class-aware focal loss with $\gamma=2.0$ to reduce the dominance of numerous well-classified background faces without adding a hand-coded decision rule after prediction \parencite{lin2017focal,buda2018imbalance}. At inference, the stored feature-column order and normalisation statistics are reused; the exported face identifier, final class, and branch scores preserve the link to the original B-Rep face. Section~\ref{sec:processing_pipeline} uses that exported link to define the conditions under which a prediction can enter a geometry operation.

\section{Feature-Processing and Training Protocol}\label{sec:processing_pipeline}

\subsection{Conservative candidate filtering}\label{sec:risk_filtering}

Conservative post-processing examines only faces predicted as targets and returns a rejected candidate to \textit{background}; it never promotes a background prediction. The rivet guard rejects a predicted rivet when its relative face area exceeds $5\times10^{-6}$. The surface-feature guard rejects only the specified wing or shell topologies computed from face geometry and local adjacency; for the original-model surface-feature stage, an exterior-visibility check also rejects a candidate when none of its sampled face points has an unblocked ray along any of the six Cartesian directions. These rules restrict which predictions are sent to geometry operations after classification, and their thresholds are part of the fixed processing contract rather than evidence of operation success.

\subsection{Staged removal, reconstruction, and validation}\label{sec:geometry_protocol}

Each retained candidate carries the face identifier of the B-Rep on which it was predicted. The processing stage uses this identifier to locate the face, perform removal or local reconstruction, and preserve an auditable link to the input model. Prediction accuracy, operation success, and suitability for a particular engineering analysis nevertheless require separate checks; acceptance for simulation depends on the task, loads, boundary conditions, and regions that must remain intact \parencite{thakur2009cad_simplification,antolin2024analysis_aware_defeaturing}.

Removal or reconstruction changes topology and face enumeration, so a face identifier identifies an entity only in the model from which its prediction was generated. The workflow first removes rivet candidates, extracts faces from the intermediate model, and then predicts surface-features on that model. The second prediction uses its own set of face identifiers.

\subsection{Data Annotation and Training Protocol}\label{sec:data_training}

\subsubsection{Annotation contract and model-level partitioning}\label{sec:data_partition}

Annotation records retain each original face identifier and its source category. Rivet faces receive the \textit{rivet} label; decal and window faces share the \textit{surface-feature} label; and all remaining faces are labelled \textit{background}. The face identifier allows each label to be checked against the corresponding B-Rep model before training or evaluation.

All faces from an aircraft remain in one partition, avoiding overlap of same-aircraft faces and local adjacency relations \parencite{kapoor2023leakage}. Model parameters and normalisation statistics are computed from the training partition. The full test set is processed with the stored feature order, statistics, class mapping, and decision thresholds; it is not used to update the reported checkpoint. \section{Experimental Results}\label{sec:results}

This section evaluates the evidence chain from face-level prediction to geometric processing. The recognition results use a reproducible prediction--truth pairing in which records are aligned by face identifier. Candidate outputs and visual examples are retained to document the processing route, but they are not used as evidence of operation-level completeness.

\subsection{Face-level recognition and background-risk characterisation}\label{sec:e1_results}

The fixed checkpoint was evaluated on all 34,162 faces in the full test set. Prediction and truth files were aligned by face identifier for each model, so the class metrics and aircraft-level error counts refer to the same faces. Epoch-wise histories are unavailable, and the reported values therefore characterise the stored checkpoint rather than training convergence.

For each class, precision ($P$) is the proportion of predicted faces in that class that are correct, whereas recall ($R$) is the proportion of true faces in that class that are recovered. The F1 score is their harmonic mean, $F1 = 2PR/(P+R)$, and therefore summarises the balance between false candidates and missed target faces.

The full-test results give F1 scores of 0.9312 for rivets and 0.8091 for surface-features (Table~\ref{tab:e1_class_metrics}). Overall accuracy was 0.9900 and background F1 was 0.9949. Rivet precision and recall were 0.9436 and 0.9191, respectively. Surface-feature precision and recall were 0.7841 and 0.8358, respectively. These class-level measures complement the background-dominated overall accuracy and show the remaining trade-off between missed target faces and false candidates.

\subsection{Discussion and evidence boundaries}\label{sec:experimental_boundaries}

The experiments show that the proposed method can recognise rivet and surface-feature faces in complete-aircraft B-Rep models at the face level. On the full test set, rivet F1 is 0.9312 and surface-feature F1 is 0.8091. Each prediction retains a face identifier that links it to a locatable B-Rep entity, so the predicted faces can be used as traceable candidate inputs for staged CAD processing. For the original--simplified pairs reported in Table~\ref{tab:e3_mesh}, processing based on these candidates is associated with lower surface-triangle counts and median mesh-generation times under the tested meshing settings. The results therefore support the use of the method for traceable CAD and mesh preprocessing.

The predictions should nevertheless be reviewed before geometric operations are accepted. Residual false positives vary across aircraft models, and the surface-feature branch has higher recall than precision; consequently, some background faces remain among the candidate surface-features. Candidate counts should be interpreted as operation inputs, rather than as feature-instance counts or completed removals. Because rivet processing changes face enumeration, the model is re-extracted before the second-stage prediction, so its face identifiers refer to the intermediate model rather than the original model.

Under fixed OpenCASCADE controls, the pairs reported in Table~\ref{tab:e3_mesh} have lower surface-triangle counts and median meshing times after simplification. The changes in triangle count and time are not proportional, indicating that triangle count alone does not determine meshing time. At matched triangle targets, Table~\ref{tab:qem_matched_time} also shows lower final-meshing components than original meshing plus the QEM kernel, under the explicitly limited timing accounting. These results describe CAD-face and mesh-scale changes under the stated settings; they do not establish end-to-end timing superiority, geometric fidelity, B-Rep equivalence, or preservation of analysis response.

\section{Conclusions}
This study developed a framework for face-level identification of rivet and surface-feature faces in B-Rep models of complete aircraft, while preserving their use in subsequent geometric operations. Each face and its shared-boundary relations are encoded as a graph, and two class-specific GNNs produce the three-class output. Retaining the original face identifier in every exported prediction maintains a direct link between the learned label and an editable B-Rep entity, allowing candidate faces to be located and reviewed on the relevant original or intermediate model before local processing.

On the full test set of 34,162 faces, the framework achieved F1 scores of 0.9312 for rivets and 0.8091 for surface-features. For original--simplified pairs, feature processing reduced surface-triangle counts by 0.57\%--48.66\% and median mesh-generation times by 2.49\%--58.29\% under the same meshing settings. At matched triangle targets, final meshing after B-Rep processing was 6.89\%--59.99\% lower than the separately timed original-meshing-plus-QEM-kernel component. These results define the practical value of connecting recognition with traceable geometric processing. Locatable predictions support reviewable CAD modifications, while lower mesh complexity and shorter meshing times can improve the efficiency of simulation-model preparation and design iteration. The measured benefit is confined to preprocessing; solver runtime and engineering response were not evaluated.

The present evidence does not establish transferability to other CAD sources, complete feature-removal success, end-to-end timing superiority over QEM, geometric fidelity, or equivalence for a downstream CAE task. Candidate-face counts are not operation success rates. Future work should evaluate external CAD sources, record the outcome of each removal and reconstruction attempt, compare both routes using matched quality criteria and complete timing, isolate component effects through repeated controlled experiments, and assess task-specific CAE responses under defined loads and boundary conditions. These extensions are required to determine when the geometric changes are acceptable for a particular analysis purpose.
